# The Magic Hower

## Author: Joel L. Caesar

# Chapter 1

## "The Mage Who Didn't Need a Wand"

Oserean is not a city of castles, parapets, and giant walls. It still has tall towers, medieval in nature, and occasionally some other castle-like accouterments. Mostly, it is filled with one to two-story homes and business buildings no taller than three. Wide-paved streets lead up to The Ingenium Conservatory, the pride of the city of Oserean. It stands in the center and resembles a battle-hardened fortress behind ramparts, anything but a castle. Streets are littered with students making their way to the Conservatory.

The streets themselves are laid in fitted stone, each block cut close enough that a coin dropped between two of them would need luck as much as a steady hand to recover, yet the fit is precise rather than perfect, worn smooth by centuries of boots and cart wheels alike. Closer to the harbor, the paving grows older still - narrower lanes crook and double back on themselves beneath low, dark eaves and gapstone walls laid down before anyone thought to widen a road for anything larger than a handcart, a hold-over from whatever Oserean was before it decided to become a capital. It is there, more than anywhere else in the city, that a walker can feel how much time the place has actually seen, stone stacked on stone stacked on stone until the foundation itself becomes a kind of memory. Morning brings the city up in stages: bakers first, their ovens breathing warm yeast and butter out into the cool air long before the sun clears the rooftops, then the market stalls unfolding awnings over crates of fruit and root vegetables, then finally the carts - wooden wheels grinding over stone, harness bells, the occasional stubborn horse refusing to be hurried - as vendors haggle and hawkers work their routes with just enough persistence to be heard and not enough to be resented. There are no engines here, no hover-lanes threading between buildings the way Earth cities wear them; Oserean gets by on hooves and legs and the occasional cart, and nobody who lives here misses what they've never had. For the shopkeeper unlocking his door before dawn, for the baker's wife setting out the first tray, for the old woman walking the same three streets to market and back as she has for forty years, this is simply a Tuesday - the Conservatory a fixed landmark at the center of things, its bells marking time the way any other clock tower might, its students a familiar current running through streets that belonged to the city long before any of them arrived and will belong to it long after they've gone.

The morning sun cast long shadows across the cobblestone courtyard of The Ingenium Conservatory, where students hurried with their scrolls and satchels. The air buzzed with the faint hum of magic - dangerous as it was beautiful. This was no ordinary school. It was a place where the rules of reality bent under the whims of those who wielded power, and death lurked in every corner, waiting for a misplaced spell or a moment of carelessness.

The Conservatory's obligatory uniforms were the closest thing the school had to a standard, and students wore them with the kind of half-hearted compliance that immediately became its own variety of expression. Boys and some girls layered button-down Oxford shirts in a scattering of colors beneath vest-like coats with long tails, the cut borrowing more from symphony conductors than anything practical, giving the assembled crowd a parade-ground rigidity that the rest of the morning quickly unraveled. Those girls who hadn't gone the coat-and-tails route had opted for the admittedly more feminine alternative: matching Oxford shirts underneath pleated skirts that fell somewhere between the knee and just below it, long enough to run and jump in without incident — and long enough, by design, to conceal the shorts everyone wore underneath without making it obvious that everyone wore them.

The courtyard had fractured itself into whatever shapes eighteen-year-olds could arrange when left to their own devices. A knot of them huddled beneath the arched colonnade on the eastern side, half in shadow, trading stories loud enough to carry across the cobblestones and loose enough in the telling that nobody present could have said which parts had actually happened.

Socializing here rarely meant talking about magic at all - after nine months of incantations and mana theory, most conversations gravitated toward the same things they might have anywhere else: family letters from home, whichever Earth show had been smuggled in on someone's last visit across the portal, whose Focus Selection interview had gone badly, and who was still avoiding whom after last term's Convocation Hall gossip. A handful of older traditions clung to the courtyard the way ivy clings to stone - nobody remembered exactly how they started, only that they were followed. First-years were told, half in jest and half in warning, never to lay a bare hand against the runed torches lining the colonnade unless they meant it; the story went that the wall itself could tell the difference between a student testing their nerve and one who actually belonged there, and nobody wanted to find out which the stone thought they were in front of an audience. It was the sort of superstition that separated the students who had only heard about magic's teeth from the ones who had already felt them - the newly arrived treated every corner of the Conservatory as a dare, while those who'd survived a term or two gave the old stonework a wide, respectful berth.
Two students stood near the fountain, circling each other in the loose stances of people about to prove something without saying what. One flicked a spark from his fingertips at the other's shoulder; the second retaliated with a ribbon of blue light that curled around the first's ankle like a pet and then dissipated into nothing.

They were playing, in Alec's terminology -- throwing harmless little spells at each other the way younger kids threw rocks, testing ranges, measuring reactions, pretending the danger was a costume they could take off when the bell rang. It wasn't entirely pretend. A misfired heat canter would leave a third-year permanently convinced his eyebrow lived somewhere near his mouth. But the courtyard held them, mostly, and the Convocation Hall had only just dismissed them, so for now the risk was small enough to be entertaining.

Alec walked alone through the crowd, his hands shoved deep into the pockets of his uniform. It was a perfectly standard affair - same Oxford shirt, same tailored coat as everyone else's - and that was precisely the point. He had worn it correctly, buttoned to the top, every line sharp and unexceptional, because blending in came as naturally to him as standing out did to the rest of them.

Magic wasn't something he used; it was something he was. To him, it was as unremarkable as the sun rising in the sky. Yet here he was, just another face among hundreds trying to disappear in plain sight at a school that seemed determined to make sure he never succeeded.

No one entered The Ingenium Conservatory at eighteen without already knowing magic - not even those who stumbled onto it late or lacked a bloodline to guide them. Power came in every measure: some students could coax sparks from nothing with a thought, others needed years of practice before they could light a candle reliably. But power was power regardless of quantity, and the Conservatory expected everyone to have at least a thread of it woven into their bones by the time they crossed the gates. Power was one thing. Knowing what to do with it was another.

He paused by a flickering torch on the wall, his gaze drifting over the runes etched into the stone. Without thinking, he reached out and brushed his fingers against one of the symbols. The torch flared to life at his touch, blazing gold. A murmur rippled through the nearby students, but he barely noticed. Magic wasn't something to be marveled at - it was simply there.

"Extraordinary," a voice purred from behind him. Alec turned to see Sage Winslow, a tall, slender girl with blonde hair cascading down her back. Her eyes gleamed with a mixture of curiosity and disdain, as though she had just discovered a particularly interesting insect. "You don't even need a wand, do you?"

Alec shrugged, his expression unreadable. "I've never needed one."

The girl's mouth curved into something like a smile. "Well, well. You must be some kind of prodigy. Or perhaps you're just... different." She let the word hang in the air, as though it carried an unspoken accusation.

Alec didn't flinch. He had heard that before, countless times. To him, being different was simply a fact of life. It wasn't something to be ashamed of - or proud of, for that matter. It just was.

As the girl walked away, her hips swaying with an exaggerated confidence, Alec turned back to the torch. The flames held steady, golden and hot against the stone. He stared at them for a moment longer before turning and disappearing into the crowd, his boots echoing silently against the stone floor.

# Chapter 2

## "The Girl Who Tamed the Beast"

Los Angeles - 2247. Aerilyn stood at eighteen for the first time in a world that wasn't hers, and she still hadn't stopped looking for the edge of it. The Conservatory's grounds stretched beyond her like something from an old dream - stone walls covered in moss, twin moons hanging low above a canopy too dark to be real - but none of it had become less impossible just because she was older now. All she could remember clearly was what happened thirteen years ago, when she was five and the world split open behind her without warning.

Thirteen years earlier.

The forest breathed. That was the first thing Aerilyn noticed when she stepped through the portal - not the magic, not the strange twin moons hanging above the canopy, but the living rhythm of it all. The trees exhaled in slow, steady pulses like lungs. The air tasted different here: richer, charged with something electric and wild that set every hair on her arms upright. She was five years old and she had never seen anything that wasn't made of concrete or steel or the flat gray screens of a world that didn't know magic existed.

She stumbled forward onto moss so thick it swallowed sound, and the portal snapped shut behind her with a sharp crack like a thunderclap. For a moment there was only silence - the kind of silence you feel in your teeth rather than hear with your ears. Then came the wind, rushing through the canopy above in waves that made the leaves flash silver on their undersides. Aerilyn stood alone at the edge of a narrow game trail, her small hands shaking, her t-shirt soaked from running and clinging to her shoulders.

Behind her: nothing. The portal was gone. Los Angeles - the crowds, the screaming, the stampede through the downtown terminal she had chased for three hours while adults pushed past her with their suitcases and their blinders on - was gone too.

She didn't cry. She had cried in Los Angeles, during the first hour when she realized her mother's hand wasn't there anymore to grip, but now there were no tears left.

The portal hadn't simply shut - it had come apart, the way a knot unravels when the string finally gives: the doorway's edges rippling inward first, folding on themselves like paper curling near a flame, before the whole opening puckered down to a single bright point and vanished with a sound too large for the space it left behind. The air around it had gone cold in the half-second before it closed, cold enough that Aerilyn's breath fogged in front of her even though it had been summer only a heartbeat earlier on the other side, and then just as quickly the cold broke and the humid green warmth of the trees rushed back in to fill the space where thunder had been. She had smelled something sharp and metallic in that instant, like a struck match crossed with the ozone hum of a downed power line, gone before she could think to be afraid of it. Whatever had opened that doorway for her did not want to be found again; it left nothing behind to prove it had ever been there at all, only the ringing in her ears and the impossible quiet of a forest that had no idea a five-year-old girl had just fallen out of the sky into it.
Instead she walked. The trail wound deeper into the trees and opened onto a clearing where the light fell through the branches in thick golden shafts. Somewhere in the distance something called - low, resonant, almost musical - and Aerilyn stopped because the sound was beautiful in exactly the way danger sometimes is: not a warning but an invitation to pay attention.

At the clearing's far edge stood a single stone, waist-high to an adult, worn smooth and strange in a way that had nothing to do with weather - its surface swirled with faint, pale striations that seemed to shift color depending on the angle of the light, though Aerilyn was too young and too frightened to wonder why a rock might do that. She would not think of it again for thirteen years. In the moment, it was simply a shape in the clearing, no more remarkable than the trees around it, and she gave it no more attention than a child gives anything that isn't currently trying to kill her.

The Aebgarmr came out of the brush on her left like it had been there all along, materializing from shadow and foliage with that same terrible grace. It stood twice her height at the shoulder, a mass of corded muscle wrapped in mottled gray hide that shifted color as it moved - darker where the shadows pooled, lighter where sunlight caught its back. Its head was long and narrow, eyes forward-set and effervescent blue, mouth full of teeth designed for tearing through bone. The mane running from skull to spine bristled up as it spotted her: black, coarse, and bristling with awareness. It didn't snarl - no gesture needed.

Aerilyn's breath caught in her throat but she didn't run. Something in the way that creature carried itself - not frantic or confused, but deliberate and certain - told her this was exactly what it had come here to do. She had seen enough animals at the zoo, on screens, in the broken cages of a world falling apart, to recognize confidence when she saw it.

This thing knew what it was. It knew what it was going to do with her. And at that moment, the beast lunged forward. It stood so high above Aerilyn that its open jaws descended from directly overhead like a junkyard crane, all shadow and teeth closing in from the canopy's light-flecked darkness. She reached up - not away, not back, but straight toward it with both arms extended, palms facing upward as if to hold something heavy but manageable. The beast's head continued its downward arc; her hands pressed against the edges of those jaws without actually touching them, and still the mouth was forced wider by an indistinguishable force emanating from nowhere Aerilyn could name. She was holding the jaws of the beast at bay - not with strength or magic she knew how to describe - but simply because that's what it took to stop something that wanted very much to end her.

The second figure arrived from the opposite side of the clearing without warning. Aeribeth - ten years old, tall for her age, by dwarf standards, with dark hair braided close against her skull and eyes that missed nothing - came bouncing out of the trees like a stone skipping across water. She didn't shout. She didn't cast a spell. She threw two enchanted knives with her right hand in quick succession, and both struck the Aebgarmr's forelegs just above the joints: clean, precise, devastatingly accurate for someone who had never missed a target.

The beast roared - a sound that shook leaves from the branches overhead - and stumbled sideways as magic flared hot at the impact points. The knives sank deep into muscle and released their charge in short bursts of disorientation: nerve signals scrambled, balance shattered, everything the Aebgarmr knew about its own body turned to static for half a heartbeat. It fell hard onto one knee, shaking its head violently, jaws snapping at nothing as confusion swept through it like a fever.

Aeribeth landed between the beast and Aerilyn in three quick strides, her ten-year-old frame tense and ready, both hands already reaching for more knives from the bandolier across her chest. She turned her head just enough to look back at the human child with one eye: "Stay behind me."

Aerilyn didn't move. She watched the Aebgarmr struggle to find its footing again, watched the way it pawed at the ground with cloven hooves as if the earth itself had betrayed it, and something in her chest did a strange flip that wasn't fear exactly. It was closer to recognition: this thing wasn't attacking because it was angry or malicious. It was doing what it was built to do - hunting - and nothing more complicated than that lived inside its head.

The Aebgarmr found its balance eventually, shaking itself in a slow rolling motion from spine to tail-tip. The knives' effects were fading. It turned those forward-set blue eyes toward Aeribeth and snarled - a low, guttural sound that promised violence if provoked further.

But it didn't charge. Not yet. Whatever instinct told it to hunt was tangled with another instinct: caution. This small creature had hurt it without making noise or waving sticks or doing any of the things prey normally did to announce itself. That made it dangerous. That also made it worth thinking about.

Aeribeth held her position, daggers ready in both hands now, shoulders hunched and breathing shallow. She was terrified - Aerilyn could see it in the tightness around her eyes, in the way her fingers trembled just slightly against the knife handles - but she didn't back down an inch.

Aerilyn took a step forward. Then another.

"I'm Aeribeth," she said quietly, and for once the older girl's name came out steady.

Aeribeth glanced over her shoulder again: "Don't." The word was sharp, absolute.

But Aerilyn had already crossed the distance between them. She walked past Aeribeth's rigid form toward the Aebgarmr with the same calm determination that had carried her through a crowd of panicked adults in Los Angeles without breaking stride. The beast watched her approach - blue eyes tracking, head lowered slightly, mane still half-raised but settling as it assessed this new variable: small human child, no weapons, no magic, moving forward when every other animal would have turned and fled.

She stopped three paces away and raised both hands, palms open. The Aebgarmr's nostrils flared once, twice, sampling the air for fear or deceit or anything that might tell it what to expect next. Aerilyn offered nothing but stillness and an expression she had learned on the streets of a city that didn't care whether you made it home: curious, not afraid, slightly amused by the absurdity of the situation itself.

"We can be friends," she said to the beast, her voice carrying across the clearing with the unselfconscious directness only a child possesses - "can't we?"

The Aebgarmr stared at her for a long moment. Then it lowered its head slowly until its muzzle was level with her hands and let out a breath that warmed the air between them. Its blue eyes had lost their hostility now, just the dull caution of something that had been hurt and didn't understand why. Aerilyn reached forward and placed both palms against the coarse black fur at the base of its mane. The beast didn't flinch. It leaned into her touch - a heavy, deliberate motion that nearly knocked her backward, but she held her ground and gripped the thick hair with both hands anyway.

Then it licked her hand: broad, rough strokes across her palm and fingers, tasting her the way animals do when they decide someone isn't food. The sensation was surprisingly gentle for something built to crush bone. Aerilyn laughed this time - not the tight nervous sound she'd made in Los Angeles, but an open one that rang through the clearing and made even Aeribeth lower her daggers just enough to breathe.

"Okay," Aeribeth said from behind them, her voice quiet now with something approaching disbelief. "That's it? You're not going to eat her?"

The Aebgarmr made a low rumbling sound in its chest - not a growl but something closer to agreement - and nudged Aerilyn's shoulder with the side of its head, nearly toppling her onto the moss. She caught herself against its neck and held on, grinning up at the impossible gray wall of muscle above her.

Aeribeth holstered her daggers and walked forward slowly, cautiously, until she stood beside them both. She reached out a hand to touch the Aebgarmr's mane - tentative at first, then firmer as the beast didn't react negatively. She looked down at Aerilyn with an expression that might have been pride or relief or something harder to name, and said: "You're going to cause me problems one day."

Aerilyn grinned up at her without letting go of the Aebgarmr's mane. "Maybe."

The beast shook itself again in that slow rolling motion from skull to spine-tip, then turned and ambled back into the trees with the same deliberate grace it had arrived with - pausing once at the edge of the clearing to look back over its shoulder as if checking that both small creatures were still there. They were. It disappeared into the undergrowth without a sound, leaving only flattened moss and the memory of blue eyes behind.

Aeribeth knelt down and pulled Aerilyn into an embrace so tight it knocked the air out of her. "We're going home," she whispered into the top of Aerilyn's head. "And you're never letting go of me again."

Aerilyn nodded against her shoulder, still holding onto the space where the beast had been standing, still feeling the rough warmth of its tongue on her palm. The forest breathed around them, and somewhere in the distance that same low musical call echoed through the canopy.

# Chapter 3

## "Verdicts Before Lessons"

The Convocation Hall swallowed sound the way deep water swallows light. Two hundred new students filed in beneath vaulted stone ribs that arched overhead like the inside of some vast fossilized ribcage, and their footsteps, their whispers, the creak of leather satchels - all of it thinned to nothing under that ceiling, as though the building itself demanded silence before anyone had thought to ask for it.

In full daylight the hall reveals just how old it actually is. The windows run floor-to-vault along both walls, but there is no color in them beyond parchment and pale honeyed yellow - clear enough to tell morning from afternoon at a glance, and around each pane a border of carved runes worked directly into the stone frame rather than painted on, worn soft at the edges the way river stones wear soft, their meaning lost to everyone except whichever archivist still bothers to translate dead notation for fun. Stone podiums stand at intervals along the walls, each one crowned with the bust of a past archon or principal, their expressions carved into the particular blend of severity and fatigue that seems to come standard with the office. Between them hang framed renderings not of faces but of magic circles - old defensive patterns sketched out in careful linework, the kind of thing a first-year might study before an exam if they knew to look up instead of down. And down is where the real protection lives: a faint tracery of wards cut into the floor itself, so shallow they read as decoration until a misfired spell finds one and the whole pattern flares gold for exactly as long as it takes to swallow the mistake whole. The wood paneling - maple, mostly, with a darker vein of cedar running through the joinery - keeps the room from feeling like a tomb, but centuries of robes and candle smoke and old stone have worked themselves into the grain all the same, so that even on a bright morning the air still carries a hint of something older than anyone currently breathing it.

Alec found a seat near the back, not out of shyness but out of habit - the back of any room was where he ended up, the way water finds the low ground without trying. Ars Vosse pushed past two younger students to claim a seat in the third row, close enough to be seen, and made a show of setting his wand across his knees like a man laying out silverware before a meal he intended to be photographed eating.

Archon Grok Blackwood did not walk to the podium so much as arrive at it, the way weather arrives - no fanfare, a change in the pressure of the room. He was built low and wide, like something meant to anchor a structure rather than decorate it, and in his right hand he carried a golden judge's gavel worn smooth at the head from decades of use. He set it on the podium with a single, unhurried click that carried to the back wall better than any shout could have.

"Magic is dangerous," he said, without preamble, without welcome. "You might die before you graduate."

No one laughed. A few students glanced sideways at each other, waiting for the joke to reveal itself, but Grok's face offered nothing to soften the sentence - no dry amusement, no theatrical pause designed to let the words land before he undercut them with reassurance. He let it sit exactly as heavy as it sounded.

"I say that first because everything else I tell you today will be easier to hear once you've accepted it. This is not a place that promises you will leave. It is a place that promises you will be changed, and change, in my experience, has never once asked permission." He rested one hand on the gavel, not gripping it, simply touching it the way a man touches a doorframe he has walked through ten thousand times. "Some of you will leave this hall in four years as something unrecognizable to the person who walked in this morning. That is the point. If you wanted to remain exactly who you are, you enrolled at the wrong institution."

He let his gaze move across the rows - not scanning, not performing attentiveness, but actually looking, the way a man checks a foundation for cracks before deciding whether to build on it. Alec felt the weight of that gaze pass over him and continue on, unhurried, and understood without being able to explain how that Grok had catalogued something about him in that half-second and moved on without needing to linger.

"I have stood at this podium for longer than most of your parents have been alive," Grok continued, "and in that time I have learned that the students who survive this place are rarely the most powerful ones in the room. They are the ones who understood, early, what they were becoming - and made their peace with it before it arrived." He paused. "If there is a version of you five years from now that cannot live inside what this Conservatory offers - the discipline, the judgment, the cost - I would rather you discover that today, in this hall, than in a corridor with a spell half-cast and no one able to undo what happens next."

A murmur moved through the rows, quickly stifled. Somewhere near the front, a girl's hand crept toward her satchel strap and gripped it.

"This is not a threat," Grok said, and for the first time something almost gentle crossed his face - not warmth, exactly, but the recognition of someone who had delivered this same address enough times to know precisely which sentence needed the temperature adjusted. "It is the truest kindness I can offer you before the rest of your education begins. Power without judgment is just a quicker way to get yourself killed. We will teach you both, in this hall and outside it. But only one of those two things has ever gotten a student killed for lacking it, and it isn't the one you're expecting."

A hand rose near the middle row - Kian Thorpe, a new student with the untroubled confidence of someone who had just realized he'd been listening to the wrong sentence his entire life. "Can we leave the ground?" he called out.

Grok tilted his head. "You mean fly?"

"No, *grounds*." Kian leaned forward, emphasizing the s with the particular precision of a boy who had learned to defend his grammar in rooms where nobody else would. "As in - be allowed to go outside?"

Grok smiled then, a small, matter-of-fact crook at the corner of his mouth. "I don't care." He let it hang for a beat, watching the ripple of relief move through the rows, before continuing. "It's not that I don't care, I do. Look, you're all eighteen. If I told you not to go outside, I'd have to look for you if you did anyway, and honestly, I can't be bothered. You're not children anymore. You're young adults." His gaze drifted across the sea of faces, pausing for half a second on someone near the front whose uniform jacket was clearly worn over pajama bottoms beneath his coat. "Well, most of you are."

The hall erupted. Kian groaned at his own expense, and a few students doubled over with the particular joy of being roasted by a principal who could literally crush them with a gavel if he wanted.

"Why're you laughing?" Grok said, and for a moment the smile didn't reach his eyes, just a flash of practiced severity. "That was an insult."

Then he laughed with them - a deep, warm sound that turned the Convocation Hall inside out, the way a fortress suddenly remembered it had doors. The laughter subsided slowly, the rows still grinning as they filed for their breaks.

He lifted the gavel then, turning it once in his hand so the gold caught what little light filtered through the high windows, and set it down again - a verdict rendered on nothing in particular, or perhaps on everything at once.

"Welcome to The Ingenium Conservatory," he said. "Class begins in one hour. I suggest you spend it deciding whether you meant what you signed."

The students who'd heard him described only as a broad shape at the back of Convocation Halls finally got a proper look: bald, an overflowing mustache that seemed to have ambitions beyond anything else on his face, and a pair of eyebrows that appeared to possess both pride and a mind of their own. They moved independently of one another - arched, flat, twitching - as though each were running its own private commentary on whatever he was listening to. In any other wardrobe he'd have been mistaken easily enough for a construction worker, or a walrus. He turned from the podium without waiting for anyone to move, and those two eyebrows settled into something like approval before they disappeared from view entirely.

He stepped down from the podium without waiting to see whether anyone moved, and the hall exhaled all at once - two hundred held breaths released in a ragged wave of shuffling feet and half-whispered questions nobody wanted to be the first to ask aloud. Ars was already turning to the students on either side of him, opening his mouth around some observation Alec was too far away to hear. Alec didn't move yet. He sat with his hands loose in his lap, turning the shape of the address over the way he turned over most things - without alarm, without the flutter of fear rippling through the rows around him, just a quiet, careful attention to what had actually been said versus what everyone else seemed to be hearing.

Not as many as Grok was letting on - Alec realized it now. Not nearly enough would walk out of here alive.

After the hall emptied, Artemis Vanthe, a junior instructor, sold wands from the Conservatory store to students who'd arrived without one — which turned out to be more than expected, given how late some families waited before admitting their child needed formal training. "No refunds once class starts," he told them flatly, the way people who've explained this five hundred times explain it: with a tired finality that leaves no room for bargaining. "Test your wands now if you're unsure they work." A few students waved theirs nervously and got sparks; one got nothing at all. Vanthe peered at the wand's tip, frowned, then looked up at the student with a sigh that was almost kind. "Not broken," he said. "Just incompatible - your magic channels differently than standard wood and core. We can swap it before class starts tomorrow. After that, you're stuck with what you bought."

# Chapter 4

## "How Many Won't Make It?"

Grok's office occupied a corner of the administrative tower that received almost no natural light, which suited him. Shelves climbed the walls floor to ceiling, packed with ledgers whose spines had faded from handling rather than sun, and the golden gavel sat on the desk within easy reach, the way another man might keep a letter opener or a loaded drawer.

He looked up when the door opened without a knock, and did not immediately recognize irritation as the correct response, though it would have been the easy one. Students didn't often find their way to this office an hour after orientation. Fewer still did it on their own legs, unescorted, without an appointment slip clutched in a nervous hand.

Thirty years of use had worn the office down to exactly the shape of the man running it. The desk - oak, broad enough that two students could sit across from it without their knees touching - carried a permanent sheen worn into the wood where his forearms rested during every difficult conversation he'd ever had, a groove no amount of polish could erase. A faint tracery of warding ran along the wall behind his chair, the same pale gold thread that marked every other protected room at the Conservatory, though his had been recut and refreshed so many times over the decades that the newer lines sat slightly proud of the old, layered like rings in a tree. Beside the ledger shelves stood a low, unlocked cabinet holding a modest stack of letters - replies to families, some already sealed and stamped, others still waiting on a line he hadn't found yet, because there was no template for telling a mother her son wasn't coming home and Grok had never once pretended there was. But it was the two scrolls mounted high on the wall behind his desk that dominated the room once a visitor noticed them: taller than a man, older than anyone currently living, their surfaces alive with a slow scroll of names too numerous to read individually. In memoriam discipulorum, read one label. In memoriam facultatis, read the other. Hundreds of names passed beneath each heading in unhurried procession, the ink rewriting itself as it moved, and it was impossible to sit across that desk for more than a minute without understanding exactly what kind of arithmetic Grok had spent thirty years trying to keep in his favor.

"Alec," Grok said. Not a question. He had learned the name during the address, the way he learned all two hundred, filed and cross-referenced against faces before the students themselves had finished finding their seats.

"You said not everyone leaves the way they came in." Alec stood in the doorway, hands in his pockets, unhurried. "How many don't leave at all?"

Grok set down the ledger he'd been annotating and studied him - not with the sharp, assessing flick he'd given the assembled rows that morning, but slower, more deliberate, the way a man studies a structure he's been told is sound and wants to verify with his own hands rather than take on faith. He was accustomed to this question arriving wrapped in fear - a student who'd gone pale during the address and needed reassurance dressed up as information, or one performing bravado to mask the same fear from the other direction. This was neither. Alec's voice held no tremor and no theater. It was the tone of someone asking how far a bridge could be trusted before crossing it, nothing more, nothing less.

"Sit," Grok said, and Alec did, without the visible relief most students showed at being invited to.

"Why do you want to know?" Grok asked. "Be precise. I've had this question from frightened children and from children performing fearlessness, and I answer them differently."

Alec considered this honestly, the way he considered most things - without rushing to produce the answer that would sound best. "Because you said it like it mattered. Not like something people say to sound serious. I want to know what it actually costs to be here."

Something shifted behind Grok's eyes - not surprise, precisely, but the recalibration of a man who has just moved a piece from one category of his ledger to another. He leaned back in his chair, and for a moment the office held only the faint hum of whatever wards kept the tower's stonework from settling.

"Not as many as you might think," Grok said finally. "The Conservatory has stood for longer than either of us can comfortably imagine, and in all that time, the number who die within these walls from misused magic is smaller than the number who die from ordinary carelessness on a staircase. Alec waited. He had the patience of someone who understood that the sentence wasn't finished, that Grok had chosen his words with the specificity of a man laying stepping stones across a river rather than building a bridge outright.

He picked up the gavel, turning it once between his fingers, not for effect this time but the way a man handles something familiar while thinking. "There are students each year whose power outpaces their judgment badly enough that continuing here would endanger others more than it educates them. There are students whose minds cannot hold what advanced magic asks of them without breaking somewhere in the middle. "What happens to them instead?" Alec asked.

Grok looked at him for a long moment, and something in his expression suggested he was deciding how much further this particular stepping stone could bear weight. "Thank you," Alec said, and stood.

"You're not frightened," Grok observed, not quite a question.

"I don't think fear changes what's true," Alec said. "I just wanted to know what I was actually agreeing to."

Grok watched him go, the door easing shut behind him without a slam, and sat for a long moment with the gavel still in his hand before he returned to his ledger. It was, he reflected, one of perhaps a dozen times in his tenure that he had explained himself to a student outside a lecture hall, and the only time in recent memory he'd done it without being asked twice.

## "The Tenebrous Vivarium"

The Tenebrous Vivarium sits at the north end of the Conservatory grounds, mostly out of the way of student traffic and mostly out of their minds as well. It is a place of little admiration - a single unimpressed glance through the doors is usually enough to earn it a permanent kind of forgetting. Not that anyone forgets it exists, exactly. Nigredo-Obscurium Flux - a black liquid that reflects no light whatsoever, the closest thing to a black hole that could ever exist in bottled form. No one knows exactly where it comes from, beyond the ground itself. It isn't pumped in from some hidden well or shipped from anywhere identifiable. "See my tattoos?"

He glanced at her, then quickly looked away again.

"It's okay, you can look," she said, a smile tugging at one corner of her mouth. "They're gone."

"You can keep that," she said, smiling. Alec looked at the little ink umbrella, then at her, and let himself smile back - not embarrassed anymore, just caught, briefly, in something that felt easier than it should have.

## "Ars' Hold"

# Chapter 7

## "You Really Are an Ars"

It happened three days later, in the practice yard behind the Restraint building, where a dozen students were meant to be working through a controlled-release drill - light a candle, hold the flame at exactly one inch, extinguish it without smoke. Simple. Deliberately, punishingly simple, the kind of exercise designed to humble people who thought competence and control were the same skill.

The yard behind the Restraint building saw a dozen different drills over the course of a week, and its permanent fixtures showed it: target dummies grouped by melee and ranged discipline along the far fence, a row of iron cauldrons kept topped off for water-manipulation exercises, a modest pile of fist-sized stones nobody had gotten around to clearing since the last lifting drill. But the walls were the yard's real record-keeper - pocked with centuries of scorch marks that no repair charm ever fully lifted, dark starbursts layered over older dark starbursts until the stone looked bruised rather than burned. Underfoot was mostly packed gravel, pebble-smooth and pale, chosen so a student who overcorrected and ended up on their back would come away with wounded pride rather than a skinned knee; a strip of turf ringed the outer edge, yellowed and threadbare where one drill or another had scorched it past recovery for the season. Squat iron warding pillars stood at intervals along the walls, unglamorous as fire hydrants, each humming faintly with the same barrier magic that arched overhead in an invisible dome thirty feet up - invisible, that is, until something pushed against it, at which point it bloomed into a faint lattice of gold and absorbed the overflow rather than letting it land in someone's face. That was the thing about this yard specifically: it told you exactly how much control a student actually had, because the barrier didn't just catch mistakes, it lit up when it caught them, broadcasting a small private failure in glowing gold for anyone paying attention. A flame held at a clean, disciplined inch left the dome dark and unbothered. A flame that slipped, even for half a second, got to watch its failure flare across the sky in front of everyone.

Ars had been assigned a partner for the drill - a slight, quiet boy named Corwin who fumbled his incantation twice before managing a flame at all. Ars, wand already drawn, watched the third attempt with visibly thinning patience, and when Corwin's flame guttered a half-inch too high, Ars snatched the candle out of his hands entirely.

"Like this," he said, and it wasn't instruction so much as confiscation. He snapped off the drill's incantation with crisp, showy precision, holding the flame at a flawless inch for a beat longer than necessary before pinching it out between two fingers with a flourish. "See? Wasn't hard."

Corwin's ears went red. He reached for the candle and Ars held it a half-second out of reach - not cruel, exactly, just careless in the specific way of someone who had never once had to consider what it cost the other person to watch their own task performed better, faster, and taken from them in the same motion.

Alec was across the yard, arms folded, watching a flame of his own hold steady without having glanced at it in a full minute. He'd seen the whole exchange - the snatch, the flourish, the half-second Corwin's hand hung empty in the air. He crossed the yard unhurried, the way he crossed most distances, and by the time he arrived Corwin had already retreated a step, candle back in hand, flame guttering low.

"Ars," Alec said, conversational, almost warm. "I'm waiting."

Ars turned, wand still loose in his hand. "Waiting for what?"

"The attribution," Alec said, "of the most horrible retribution upon your soul." He let it sit there, perfectly flat, perfectly serious, the kind of sentence that could have belonged in one of Grok's ledgers if Grok had ever developed a sense of humor about it.

A beat. Corwin, despite himself, made a small strangled sound that might have been a laugh trying not to be one. Ars opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again - visibly hunting for the shape of a comeback and finding the cupboard empty.

"You really are an ars," Alec said, flat, unhurried, no malice in it and none needed - the observation had done all the work on its own, the way it always did, riding on nothing but Ars's own name and Ars's own three days of giving it fresh material.

This time the laugh that went around the yard didn't bother hiding itself. Even Corwin grinned, candle forgotten, flame finally guttering out on its own with nobody paying it any attention at all. Ars's face went through several colors in quick succession before settling on the deep, resigned red of a man who has recognized, somewhere around the fourth repetition, that a nickname stops being a joke and becomes a fact of the landscape.

"That's not even clever anymore," he muttered, shoving his wand back into his belt.

"It's exactly as clever as it was the first time," Alec said. "You just keep giving me the setup." He held out a hand toward Corwin's candle, not touching it, and the flame steadied itself an inch above the wick without any incantation at all, holding perfectly still.

Then he turned and walked back across the yard, and Ars was left standing beside a boy whose candle now burned steadier than his own, turning the shape of the insult over in his mind and finding, infuriatingly, that it fit.

# Chapter 10

## "Aethel-Singer"

Rorie's forge sat beyond the Terradin foothills, half-buried into a mountainside where the rock itself seemed to remember being molten. Aeribeth found it on a weekend pass — past the last of the Demesne's residential tunnels, down a trail marked only by the faintest hum in the air that made her teeth ache if she stood too close and listened too long. She followed that sound instead, because when you'd spent thirteen years learning to trust your instincts over maps, instinct was where you started.

The entrance was a stone archway carved with runes so old they'd been smoothed by centuries of dwarven hands - not decorative marks but the kind of inscriptions Rorie used like tuning forks. Aeribeth pushed through and stepped into a cavern that shouldn't have existed: wide as a cathedral, lit from below by coals that glowed without smoke, and warm in a way that had nothing to do with fire.

The first thing she noticed was the metal.

It sat on a workbench near the back of the forge — a bar roughly the length of her forearm, dark at rest but alive with light when the coals caught it from just the right angle. It wasn't reflective like polished steel; it amplified whatever illumination it received and sent it back brighter than what it had taken in. The closest thing Aeribeth had ever seen on Earth footage were those high-visibility orange strips construction workers wore, except this was metal — cold, solid, and humming with a frequency she could feel behind her eyes.

Aethelite. She'd known the name since she was old enough to hold one of her knives. It was alive - not in a biological sense, but in the way a held breath is alive, full of potential energy waiting for someone to release it.

"You're early," Rorie said from somewhere behind the forge bellows. She didn't turn around when Aeribeth spoke; she was already working something with her hands that made the air ripple in small concentric waves, like sound given visible form.

Rorie "Whisperstep" Dunalban - though most of the Demesne knew her by her second nickname, one she'd earned through decades of stubborn persistence rather than stealth: Aethel-Singer. Tame was probably the wrong word for what she did with Aethelite. You didn't tame a thing that refused to be tamed. This was more like a negotiation between equals - mutual respect forged over years of failing, trying again, and slowly learning the metal's language one harmonic note at a time.

"I brought my knives in for inspection," Aeribeth said, setting them on the workbench beside the raw bar. "Figured it'd be good to know if they're holding up."

Rorie turned then, wiping her hands on a leather apron that had seen more heat than most people's entire wardrobes. She was small even for a dwarf - compact and solid, with knuckles scarred from decades of close calls between hammer and metal. Her eyes flicked to the knives, then back to Aeribeth, and something like approval crossed her face.

"Good," she said simply. "They'll hold. Aethelite doesn't dull, it doesn't shatter against hard targets, it doesn't nick or dent or scratch - once I shape it into an object, that's the end of it. Aeribeth nodded.

Rorie set down her apron and walked to a stone plinth near the center of the forge - roughly waist-high, flat as glass on top, with faint grooves carved into its surface like musical notation. She placed one hand on it, closed her eyes, and began to hum.

Rorie's voice found a frequency that made the air visible: thin concentric rings rippling outward from her mouth like ripples across still water. The Aethelite bar on the workbench responded almost instantly — not melting, not bending, but shifting its molecular structure under the acoustic pressure until it softened enough to flow like thick honey while retaining its solid form.

Rorie sang for another minute, adjusting pitch and volume with the precision of a surgeon choosing an incision angle, then stopped. The air settled. The bar on the bench had changed shape: one end curved slightly upward into a smooth arc, as if it had remembered what it wanted to be and finally agreed.

"That's how I do it," Rorie said, brushing her hands together. "Acoustic frequencies — harmonic magic layered over precise chant work. You can't force Aethelite with brute strength the way you'd hammer iron or steel. It resists that. Refuses to change shape under pressure. But find its natural resonance and it'll flow like water for anyone who knows how to ask." She met Aeribeth's eyes. "The metal chooses who listens to it, not the other way around. I've studied Aethelite for decades — thirty-seven years, to be exact — and there are maybe six dwarves on this continent who can do what I just did. The rest of us spend our whole lives trying."

Aeribeth picked up one of her knives from the bench and held it up to the light. The blade caught the glow from the coals and threw it back brighter than she expected — a chrome-shine that seemed to pull illumination from everywhere at once, even in shadows where no direct light reached. It was beautiful in a way she'd never quite articulated before: not brilliant, necessarily, but shiny. A shine that lived on its own, amplifying whatever brightness existed around it until the blade practically lit itself.

"Aen's staff is made of this," Rorie said, reading Aeribeth's expression with the practiced ease of someone who had spent her life watching people react to metal. "The Aegis Clutch. Diamond-studded ferrule, articulated claw finial — all Aethelite underneath. Before that staff, Aen burned through more staves than she can count. Every single one she tried couldn't handle her power. The diamond cracked, the mount warped, the channeling collapsed under the strain of holding back catastrophe after catastrophe." She smiled faintly. "The Aegis Clutch is the first one to survive. Once I set it into shape, it became unchangeable — no nicks, no divets, no scratches, no cracks. The metal holds her power without degrading at all. It's my highest achievement."

Aeribeth turned the knife over in her hands, feeling its balance. "How did you get the material? I always figured smithing was the Demesne's whole reason for sitting on top of a mountain full of ore. Wouldn't Aethelite come from down there with everything else?"

Rorie laughed — a short, dry sound that carried more humor than amusement. "Smithing is our province, girl, don't mistake me. Every seam of iron and copper and tin under these hills belongs to a dwarf who knows exactly how to pull it free and shape it into something useful. That part's true enough, and you'd be right to assume it about anything else we forge." She set down her tools and folded her arms. "But not Aethelite. That's not a mountain metal at all. Aeribeth felt something shift behind her eyes — a memory surfacing from thirteen years ago, when she was ten and throwing enchanted knives at a monster twice her height to save a five-year-old girl who'd stumbled out of a portal into the wrong forest. Clawde's jaws had unhinged to 180 degrees, supernaturally bright eyes fixed on Aerilyn with cold intent before Aeribeth's blades found their mark. "Exactly." Rorie's expression was unreadable for a moment — not quite fondness, not quite guilt, something in between that Aeribeth couldn't name. "Clawde needs to behave himself and stay in my good graces because I need him alive. You can't hunt an Aebgarmr — no one has ever successfully hunted one. The ones that have been tried either died trying or came away with enough scars they never fought again. So the only way to get Aethelite is waiting for an Aebgarmr to pass away naturally, which isn't something that happens frequently given how few of them exist on this planet."

She leaned against the workbench, crossing her arms. "Mages covet the material - every one of them swears it channels magic better than iron or steel even though it has no intrinsic enchantment whatsoever. It's just metal. Beautiful, resonant, volatile metal that refuses to be shaped by anyone who doesn't earn its respect, but metal nonetheless. The channeling comes from whoever holds it, not the material itself."

She pushed off the bench and picked up one of Aeribeth's knives, turning it over in calloused hands with the careful attention of someone who knew exactly what she was holding. "Which is why these matter so much," she said, handing them back. "You don't just wield Aethelite - you *are* its focus. The metal responds to your intent like water responds to gravity. No enchantment bound into the steel; the magic lives in you, not in the blade."

Aeribeth nodded slowly, feeling the weight of those words settle between them like something permanent.

Rorie set down another knife and looked past Aeribeth toward the forge entrance, where afternoon light was filtering through a gap in the stone ceiling. "When Alec turned seventeen, I gifted him his wand - solid rod of Aethelite, no enchantment, pure material." She smiled faintly at the memory. "He doesn't channel magic through it - never has, never will - but even for someone who doesn't need an anchor, a stick made of this stuff is useful. Handy tool for calibrating corneas."

Aeribeth blinked. "You gave Alec his wand?"

"Someone had to," Rorie said. "He didn't ask for one and he certainly didn't need one, but it's nice to have something that catches light when you're walking through a dark corridor at three in the morning." She paused. "Aleron Vance's cane has a tip made of Aethelite too - no one knows who made it, and he swears by it. The man uses it as both physical aid and magical focus, never drawing attention to it during conversation but everyone paying close attention when he does. That's the kind of thing you get with this metal: quiet power that speaks for itself."

She straightened up and clapped her hands once - a sharp crack that made Aeribeth flinch. "Right. Your knives are fine. They've got another ten years in them, probably more if you don't do anything stupid like throw them at something they weren't designed to cut." A beat. "You know what I mean."

Aeribeth smiled despite herself. "I'll keep that in mind."

She left the forge as dusk was settling over the foothills, the hum of Aethelite still ringing faintly behind her ears - not a sound so much as a memory of one, the kind you carried into the next room and forgot about until something reminded you it was there. On the walk back, she ran her fingers absently along the edge of one knife and felt the impossible smoothness of metal that would never dull, never scratch, never wear down.

## "Three Anchors"

Preceptor Tyler Ashworth's classroom sat on the third floor of the eastern wing, its windows angled to catch the morning light in a way that made the chalk dust hang visible in the air like something suspended in water. He was young enough that half the room still occasionally forgot to call him Preceptor, and he had a habit - unconscious, persistent - of letting his hand drift toward the wand at his belt whenever he was about to say something important.

"Today," he said, chalk in hand, "we talk about anchors." He drew three points on the board, spaced evenly, and connected them into a rough triangle. "Every mage, without exception, channels their power through one of three things. Sometimes more than one. Rarely none."

He tapped the first point. "Tool. A wand, a dagger, a staff - something external that gives your magic a shape to travel through. Most of you were handed a wand before you were handed a book, and there's a reason for that. A tool doesn't make you powerful, but it makes power legible. Predictable. It's the reason standard casting can be taught in a classroom at all - because the tool does half the translating between what you want and what happens."

He tapped the second point. "Incantation. Words. Not decoration - structure. An incantation is a promise you make to your own power about what shape it's going to take before it takes it. Some of you will find, over the next four years, that your incantations matter more than your wand does. Some of you will find the reverse."

A hand went up near the front - Petrin, notebook already dense with cramped handwriting. "What if you need both? Like - actually need them. Not just prefer them."

Tyler's eyes flicked, just for a fraction of a second, toward the back row, where Ars sat with his wand already resting across his desk, unconsciously arranged at a perfect right angle to his notebook. "Then you need both," Tyler said, evenly, no judgment in it. "That's not a weakness, it's a description. Some mages require the tool and the words, together, simultaneously, and their casting suffers the instant either one is missing. It's a narrower channel than some people get, but a narrow channel that always works is worth more than a wide one you can't trust."

Ars sat up a fraction straighter at that, some of the tension easing out of his shoulders, and made a small satisfied note in his own margin.

Tyler tapped the third point last, and here his hand drifted, again, toward his belt - an old habit reasserting itself even though what he was about to describe had nothing to do with the wand riding there. "And then there's the mage themself. No tool. No incantation. Just belief and imagination doing the entire job, start to finish, with nothing external to lean on. It's the rarest anchor by a wide margin, and it's the one this Conservatory spends the least time teaching, because there's very little to teach. You either find your way into it, over years, sometimes decades - or someone is simply born standing there already, and the rest of us spend our careers trying to understand how."

He didn't look at Alec when he said it, which several students noticed anyway, and Alec, near the window, didn't react at all - no pride, no discomfort, just the same unbothered attention he gave most things.

"I want you to think, for a moment, about where you sit on this triangle," Tyler said, setting the chalk down. "Not where you'd like to sit. Where you actually are, right now, today." He let the silence stretch, and around the room it did visibly different work on different faces. Some students eased back in their chairs, shoulders dropping, the particular relief of people who'd just been told, formally, that the thing they already had was enough - a wand steady in hand, an incantation memorized cold, nothing more required of them than what they'd already brought through the gates. Others went rigid instead, spines straightening, eyes fixed on the triangle on the board as though staring hard enough might reveal a fourth point they'd been quietly hoping existed - some students doing the private arithmetic of what they lacked, and not liking the sum.

"There's no wrong answer today," Tyler added, watching the room do exactly what he'd expected it to do, having done the same arithmetic himself once, a long time ago, in a classroom not unlike this one. "There's only an honest one. We'll spend the next four years arguing about whether you can move along this triangle at all. For now, I just want you to know where you're standing."

The chalk dust hung in the light a while longer, and nobody in the room reached for a wand.

## "The Barrier at the Gate"

Fellow Leo's classroom smelled faintly of ozone that morning, a leftover from whatever the previous class had been drilling. She stood at the front with her wand tucked into her belt rather than her hand, which the students had learned by now meant she intended to talk rather than demonstrate.

"Portals," she said, without preamble, "have no offense and no defense of their own. None. They are simply doorways - magical entities that grant passage through the grace of their own presence, nothing more. They don't fight back. They don't need to." She let that sit a moment. "Which is exactly the problem, isn't it."

A hand went up near the middle row. Leo nodded at it before Sage Winslow had even finished raising it.

"If they can't defend themselves," Sage asked, "how do you stop someone from just walking through wherever they like?"

"You don't stop the portal from being a portal," Leo said. "You build around it. Every portal has its own character, depending on how it came to be. Natural ones are hidden passages - you'd walk past a hundred of them in your life and never know it, never feel a thing, unless the world decided you were meant to find one. Constructed portals take on something of whoever built them. Some have almost no personality at all - the Pacific Coast Highway crossing is about as imaginative as a filing cabinet - and others are, frankly, showing off." A small, dry smile. "Mages built those particular ones. Draw your own conclusions."

A hand shot up near the middle row - Petrin again, notebook already dense with cramped handwriting. "If circles protect them, how come everyone's fireballs sound different? I learned 'Ignis' in my first week. Ars just yells 'Mini sun!' and makes a bigger one. How do we even know what to say if every mage uses their own words?"

Leo smiled — not the smile of someone who hadn't heard that question before, but the kind of smile someone gives when they're glad about it. "Because incantations aren't spells," she said. "They're keys. A key doesn't create a lock; it opens one that's already there. 'Ignis' works for me because my magic recognizes it. Ars's 'Mini sun' works for him because his magic recognizes that instead. Neither is right or wrong — they just mean two different mages have built their power to respond to different sounds. By the end of this year, you'll each know what words unlock your own magic." She set down her chalk. "The question isn't why everyone's incantations differ. The question is whether yours actually work when you need them to."

She turned and drew two concentric circles on the board, then a third inside them. "Here is the difficulty. A portal cannot simply be closed or switched off like a door. It is a living thing, in its way, and like any other living thing, it does not care to be hurt or killed. So if you want to protect one - and every side of every argument on both worlds currently wants to protect the ones they rely on - you protect it the way you would protect a person. Not by controlling it. By standing between it and whatever means it harm."

"That's the circle work," said Kian Thorpe from near the window, half a question.

"That's the circle work," Leo agreed. "Concentric layers of shielding magic, anchored directly into the bedrock around the portal site itself. And paired with something rather more familiar to those of you who've spent any time on Earth - a magical equivalent of an airport scanner, set at both ends of the crossing. Anyone attempting passage without proper authorization is turned around instantly. Not stopped, not questioned. Simply redirected, mid-step, back out the way they came - whether they started on Earth or on Aeriden."

"What happens to the people it turns away?" Kian asked. "If they had a real reason to cross?"

Leo's expression didn't change, but something in her voice did - a flattening, the particular carefulness of someone choosing not to editorialize in front of eighteen-year-olds who would draw their own conclusions anyway. "That," she said, "is where it stops being simple. The barriers don't know the difference between a smuggler and a mother trying to reach a hospital on the other side. They only know signatures - recognized or not. Families have been separated overnight. Legitimate travelers have been held for hours while the circles scan them over and over, because the system trusts caution over speed."

She set the chalk down. "The situation on the Earth side hasn't helped matters. What passes for immigration enforcement there right now is, by any honest account, an exercise in mismanagement."

"Then why keep the barriers up at all?" Sage asked, finally.

"Ask ten people, you'll get ten answers," Leo said. "Some will tell you it's cultural - that something in each world is worth protecting from the other, and the barriers are simply the shape that protection takes right now. Others - the Technologists, mostly, though not only them - will tell you the opposite: that the barriers represent everything wrong with letting magic operate outside anyone's oversight." She paused, and when she continued her tone had shifted slightly, quoting rather than opining. "Aleron Vance said something on this, before any of you were old enough to be paying attention to politics. 'Hysteria is, and always has been, the one thing that tears down cultural bridges.' I don't know that I entirely agree with the man on everything. But I haven't found a way to argue with that particular sentence."

She picked her wand back up off the desk, a small, deliberate gesture that told the room the lecture portion of the morning had ended.

"For homework," she said, "I want each of you to consider this: a barrier built to keep out harm will, by its nature, occasionally keep out the people it was meant to protect. That is not a flaw in the design. It is the entire design. Sit with that before next week."

She picked her wand back up off the desk, a small, deliberate gesture that told the room the lecture portion of the morning had ended. The bell rang - a sound the rest of the city had, after two centuries, mostly learned to live around - and students filed into hallways in search of food. Amelia lingered behind for a moment, gathering her things slowly while Kian and Sage headed toward the door without looking back.

"By the way," Amelia said as she slung her satchel over her shoulder, "I haven't seen Aeribeth since last week's practical. Is everything alright? She doesn't come to class or anything."

Sage laughed from down the hall. "Aeribeth doesn't go to school."

Amelia frowned. "What do you mean 'doesn't go'? She's... not enrolled?"

"Not even close." Sage shook her head, already walking backward toward Amelia with her usual combination of impatience and fond exasperation. # Chapter 14

## "Unrest at the Portal"

Los Angeles - 2247. The downtown portal district had never been quiet, exactly, but by the third week of the operation it had stopped sounding like a city at all and started sounding like something closer to a siege. Convoy trucks idled bumper to bumper along Figueroa, engines running for the air conditioning nobody inside them needed, while armed teams in unmarked tactical gray moved through the crowd in loose wedges, checking wrists, checking eyes, checking for the particular shimmer that marked someone who'd crossed through Aeriden soil recently enough for it to still be reading on a scanner.

Two centuries of vertical growth had turned downtown into something closer to a canyon than a skyline, glass towers stacked so tight along the Figueroa corridor that the streets below still ran the way city streets always had: hover-capable vehicles and standard wheeled ones sharing the same lanes bumper to bumper, since converting every road to hover-only would have meant abandoning half of what was still driving on it. Only the true arteries carried their own designated hover decks - the 10, the 405, the 5 - stacked two or three high above the standard lanes and marked by a soft blue guide-light strip that pulsed faster wherever congestion built up below, and even those dedicated lanes gave way once traffic funneled off the freeway, merging back down into ordinary shared streets well before anyone got close to downtown proper. The truly wealthy had stopped living at street level generations ago regardless; scattered among the towers stood a handful of platform structures, self-contained superstructures perched on four massive support legs like something between a crab and a courthouse, domed at the top and holding entire private neighborhoods - parks, restaurants, schools, all of it sealed off from whatever was happening sixty stories down. The portal district itself had none of that scale. It lived in the gaps: narrow courtyard plazas wedged between older buildings that had never been designed to host a doorway to another planet, each one framed by a reinforced stone arch that looked, at a glance, like municipal art until you noticed the checkpoint booths flanking it on either side and the faint lattice of etched runes crawling up the archway's inner face - old Aeriden warding grafted onto Earth concrete, glowing faintly gold whenever something crossed through that the scanners didn't like. It was the one part of the skyline that hadn't been built to impress anyone; it had been built to watch.

They called themselves the Earth Technologists Front, and they had stopped bothering with warrants somewhere around the second week. Freddie James, near the Seventh Street portal entrance, was zip-tied and marched back through the arch he'd walked out of that morning, his briefcase left behind on the sidewalk, no hearing, no filing, no name taken beyond whatever the scanner logged. Fiona Barkway, two blocks over, threw a burst of white flame at the team dragging her son Elliot toward the portal mouth - pure reflex, untrained, more panic than magic - and the footage that made every feed by nightfall showed only the flame, never Elliot's wrist already zip-tied behind him, never the three officers who'd surrounded a fourteen-year-old first. The caption running under it called Fiona an aggressor. It always did.

Malakai Sterling watched the footage twice through before the meeting, standing at the head of a conference table in a building with no sign on the door, and by the time he sat down across from President Colton Beck that afternoon, he had already decided the meeting was a formality he was attending out of courtesy rather than necessity.

Sterling's headquarters occupied four floors of an unmarked office tower that gave no indication, from the street, of housing anything besides some mid-tier logistics firm - which was, strictly speaking, the same lie its previous tenant had told. Inside, it looked less like a corporate floor and more like mission control on launch day: rows of workstations arranged in tiered arcs facing a wall of screens forty feet wide, tracking convoy positions through downtown, ship telemetry off San Pedro, biometric scans from every checkpoint arch in the city, all of it scrolling in real time under the hum of servers working harder than their casings were built for. Wall maps filled every remaining surface between the monitors - LA's portal district annotated in grease pencil with troop rotations and choke points, a chart of the San Pedro coastline studded with pins marking where his twelve converted vessels sat at anchor, recruitment tallies updated daily in a hand too neat to belong to anyone but a career officer who'd once had a staff to keep his paperwork honest. At the back, raised above the floor on its own platform, sat Sterling's office: floor-to-ceiling one-way glass that let him watch his operation without being watched in turn, a broad metal desk facing outward so he never had to turn his back on the room he'd built. His communications ran through a bank of secure lines humming behind the desk, encrypted and redundant and expensive - and beside all of it, deliberately, absurdly, sat a plain red telephone with a rotary dial, no screen, no encryption chip, nothing modern about it at all. He kept it there because it was the one line in the building that could not be hacked, tapped, or spoofed by anything running on a network, and because a man who trusted machines only as far as he could physically see them liked being reminded, every time he looked at his desk, why he'd built the rest of this in the first place.

"Total closure," Sterling said, without preamble, sliding a single sheet of paper across the table - not a proposal so much as a demand dressed in the shape of one. "Every portal on American soil, sealed, permanently, starting with downtown LA and San Pedro. And I want it on record that we don't negotiate with what comes through them. Full elimination protocol. Not deportation - deportation just sends the problem back to reload."

Beck didn't touch the paper. "You're asking me to authorize a policy of extermination against a population that includes American citizens, Mr. Sterling. Some of the people your teams are dragging through those arches were born three miles from where they're standing when your people grab them."

"They stopped being American citizens the moment they let something else into their blood," Sterling said, flat, unblinking, the certainty of a man who had said the sentence enough times that it no longer required conviction to deliver, only repetition.

## "Fool Around, Find Out"

That decision was the only real marker of dwarven presence out here; there were no waystones, no carved boundary posts, no shrines tucked into the granite the way an Earth trail might carry a plaque - dwarves left the wilderness alone as a point of principle, believing land that had to be decorated to be respected wasn't being respected at all. The one exception was the trail underfoot, and it was exception enough: cleared to a consistent width, the worst of the loose scree kicked off to either side, chokepoints between boulders widened just enough that five people walking abreast wouldn't have to think about where their feet went. It was the difference between land dwarves had decided to leave wild and land dwarves had decided a person needed to walk through without dying, and from ten feet off that trail in either direction, the wilderness didn't know the difference at all - the same fistgrass and bramblewood ran unbroken into granite folds where nothing native to Earth had ever needed a name.

"This is the least magical thing I've done in a year," Kian said, kicking a stone off the path with the satisfaction of someone who'd spent the morning being lectured about spell theory. "No offense to spell theory."

"None taken by spell theory," Sage said. "It can't hear you."

Amelia walked a half-step behind the other two, notebook tucked under one arm out of habit rather than necessity, taking in the terrain with the quiet attentiveness she brought to everything. "I still don't understand how you knew this trail existed," she said to Aeribeth. "It's not on any map I've seen."

"That's because the Demesne doesn't put its trails on maps," Aeribeth said. "Maps are how strangers find things."

She said it lightly, but she'd slowed her pace without deciding to, the way she always did when something at the edge of her attention didn't sit right. The wind had shifted. The scrub to their left had gone still in a way that wasn't quite natural - not the stillness of nothing being there, but the stillness of something choosing not to move.

"Everyone stop," she said.

Kian stopped mid-joke. Sage stopped without asking why, which told Aeribeth more about Sage's instincts than four years of Conservatory grades ever would. Amelia stopped last, still half a beat behind, still trusting that whatever was happening had a reasonable explanation.

It came out of the scrub the way nothing that size should have been able to move - low, six-legged, chitin the color of wet stone, covering the last twenty feet between it and the group in a burst of frantic, over-eager acceleration that looked less like a hunting predator and more like something thrilled to finally be let off a leash. Its eyes ringed the front of its head in a dark cluster, and even as it closed the distance Aeribeth couldn't have told anyone which direction it was actually looking.

"What is that," Sage said, already backing up.

"Skralloch," Aeribeth said. "Pack hunters. If you're seeing one, there are more."

There were. Four more emerged from the treeline in a loose spread, cutting off the trail behind them and angling wide around the group's flanks with a coordination that didn't match the twitchy, dog-eager way any single one of them moved. Amelia's breath caught audibly.

"Fireball," she said, wand already coming up, the word more instinct than plan. "Someone throw a fireball, they're not that close yet-"

"Amelia, don't-" Aeribeth started, but the incantation was already leaving Amelia's mouth, crisp and correct, the way every incantation Amelia had ever cast had been crisp and correct, because she was the kind of student who never let a spell fail through carelessness.

The fireball left her wand and reached the nearest Skralloch and did nothing at all.

Not fizzled. Not deflected. Nothing - no flare, no shimmer, no resistance of any kind, as though the spell had simply forgotten to happen the moment it crossed some boundary none of them could see. The Skralloch didn't so much as flinch. It kept coming, closing the last ten feet at a dead sprint, and Amelia - still holding her wand up, still half-convinced the spell had simply missed - didn't get her feet moving fast enough.

It hit her low, a scything foreleg catching her across the hip and throwing her sideways into the granite, and the sound she made wasn't a scream so much as all the air leaving her body at once.

"MAGIC'S NOT WORKING!" Kian shouted, which was either the least or most useful thing anyone could have said in that moment, and threw his own spell anyway out of pure reflex - a gust of wind that should have staggered the Skralloch nearest him and instead simply didn't arrive, the air in front of his wand going nowhere at all.

Aeribeth was already moving.

She didn't stop to understand why the magic wasn't working. She'd grown up in a world where the question that mattered was never why something was dangerous but what you did about it in the next half-second, and thirteen years of Terradin instinct didn't pause to process a mystery when a friend was down and bleeding. Her knives were in her hands before she'd finished crossing the distance to Amelia, and the first one left her fingers in a flat, hard line that caught the Skralloch standing over her across one of its six legs, folding it sideways with a wet crack of chitin.

"Don't cast!" she shouted, loud enough to carry over the chittering that had started up from the treeline - a layered, clicking sound, four or five voices overlapping into something that sounded almost like conversation. "Steel only! Steel only, do you hear me!"

She reached Amelia in three strides and put herself between the girl and the wounded Skralloch, a second knife already leaving her hand and burying itself in the joint where a foreleg met the body - not a killing blow, but enough to fold the creature's balance out from under it long enough for her third throw to open its throat clean.

Behind her, Kian had abandoned his wand entirely and was swinging a broken branch with more enthusiasm than technique, which was doing exactly nothing against a Skralloch's plating but at least kept him alive by pure chaotic luck. Sage had backed against the granite wall, wand still raised, visibly fighting the urge to cast anyway because every instinct she'd spent four years building told her that was what you did when something came at you - and visibly losing that fight, until Aeribeth's voice cut through again.

"Sage! Rocks! Throw rocks, throw your wand, throw anything that isn't magic!"

Sage threw her wand. It bounced off a Skralloch's skull hard enough to actually stagger it, which told her everything she needed to know, and she was already scrambling for something heavier before it finished reeling.

Two war-whoops arrived from up the trail before the dwarves themselves did - Brannock and one of the younger Shits, who'd fallen behind to argue about something and were now sprinting back at a dead run, axes already swinging before they'd fully arrived. Dwarven steel didn't ask questions about what kind of magic a creature was immune to. It simply did what steel had always done, and within seconds two more Skralloch were down, the pack's coordinated spread breaking apart into something closer to panic.

Aeribeth knelt over Amelia through all of it, one hand pressed hard against the girl's hip where blood was soaking dark through her uniform, knives still finding targets with the other without her ever quite looking away from Amelia's face.

"Stay with me," she said. "Look at me, not at them. You're fine. You're going to be fine."

Amelia's eyes were glassy, her breathing shallow and fast, but she found Aeribeth's face and held onto it, and that was enough - a person to focus on instead of the chittering retreating slowly back into the scrub as the last Skralloch broke off, dragging one of its wounded pack-mates with it, the whole group vanishing back into the treeline as suddenly as they'd arrived.

Silence came down over the trail in pieces - first the chittering fading, then Kian's ragged breathing, then Sage lowering the rock she'd never gotten the chance to throw.

"She's losing blood," Aeribeth said, to no one and everyone. "I need something to bind this. Now."

Sage was already tearing a strip from her own sleeve before Aeribeth finished the sentence, and between the two of them and Brannock's steadier, older hands, they got the wound bound tight enough to slow the bleeding, Amelia drifting in and out of focus but breathing, alive, present.

It was a long time before anyone spoke again.

"Magic didn't work," Kian finally said, staring at his empty hands like they belonged to someone else. "None of it. At all."

"No," Aeribeth said. She hadn't moved from Amelia's side, her hands shaking distantly in the stillness after everything that had just happened.

"How did you know?" Sage asked quietly. "That it wouldn't work. You knew before she even cast it."

"I've hunted them before," Aeribeth said. "You learn fast, or you don't get to learn twice."

Sage looked at her for a long moment - the same girl who'd once looked at Alec across a courtyard like he was something to be measured and found wanting - and there was none of that in her face now. Just something quieter. "I threw my wand at it," she said, half to herself, like she still couldn't quite believe it had worked better than the wand ever had.

"You did what you had to do," Aeribeth said. "That's the whole trick. There isn't a spell for it. There's just doing what you had to do."

Brannock hefted Amelia carefully, cradling her weight with the easy strength of someone who'd carried far heavier things through far worse terrain, and started back up the trail toward the portal crossing without waiting to be told. Kian fell into step beside him, still glancing over his shoulder at the treeline every few seconds. Sage walked closer to Aeribeth than she probably needed to, close enough that their shoulders brushed once on the narrow path, and neither of them moved away from it.

Amelia's dorm room had never held this many people at once, and it was starting to show - someone had dragged an extra chair in from the hallway, and there wasn't anywhere left to put it.

She was propped up against a stack of pillows, one hand resting flat over the bandaging at her hip, looking paler than usual but entirely herself in every way that mattered - notebook already back in her lap, because apparently near-death didn't rank high enough to interrupt her habits.

"You're supposed to be resting," Sage said, not for the first time.

"I am resting. I'm resting while writing down what happened before I forget the details." Amelia didn't look up from the page. "Someone should have an accurate record. Kian's version already has four Skralloch turning into six by dinner."

"They might've," Kian said, entirely unbothered. "I wasn't counting. I was busy not dying."

A knock at the doorframe turned everyone's head - Alec, leaning against it with his hands in his pockets, looking like he'd walked there without quite deciding to. "Heard there was a thing," he said. "Figured I should come see the girl who apparently got saved by a girl who doesn't even go here."

"She does more for this school than half the faculty," Amelia said, and there was no argument in the room, not even from Aeribeth, who was sitting on the end of the bed, quiet about being talked about.

Brannock filled most of the doorway behind Alec, ducking his head to clear the frame, a second Shit close on his heels - Hamish, knuckles still faintly bruised from the fight, carrying a small clay pot that smelled powerfully of something herbal and Terradin in origin.

"Brought you something for the hip," Hamish said, setting it on the desk without ceremony. "Works better than whatever they'll give you here. Don't ask what's in it."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good instinct." He nodded, satisfied, and found a spot to lean against the wall, arms crossed, looking at home in a room full of Conservatory students the way only a dwarf who'd been fighting alongside half of them the day before could manage.

Brannock didn't sit either. He simply stood near the foot of the bed, taking up the amount of space a person his size always took up, and studied Amelia with the same steady attention he'd have given a wound in the field. "You held together well," he said finally. "Considering."

"I mostly just laid there and bled," Amelia said.

"Aye. That's half of holding together well. The other half's not making it worse." He said it like a fact, not a compliment, which somehow made it land harder than if he'd dressed it up.

Alec had wandered over to the window, and from there he glanced at Aeribeth, still perched at the foot of the bed. "Kian's been telling the story like you grew wings," he said.

"I didn't grow wings."

"I know. I was there for the version where you didn't." A small, dry smile. "It's a better story than his, honestly."

Aeribeth didn't smile back, not quite, but something in her shoulders eased that hadn't been easy since the trail. "It wasn't anything special," she said. "Steel works. That's the whole trick."

"Steel and you," Sage said, quiet enough that it almost didn't carry, "not always the same thing as just steel."

Nobody argued with that either. The room held a moment of silence - too many people, one small dorm room, and the quiet relief of having them all still there.

"Anyway," Amelia said, breaking it, pen already moving again, "somebody needs to tell me what a Skralloch actually is, because Aeribeth keeps saying 'pack hunters' like that explains the eyes."

"It doesn't," Aeribeth admitted. "Nobody's explanation for the eyes has ever satisfied anybody."

"Comforting," Amelia said, and kept writing.

For days afterward, Rorie was bombarded with requests for small arms. Word of the trail had spread the way word always spread through the Conservatory - faster than fact could keep up with it - and by the end of the week half the students who'd never so much as touched a blade before were showing up at her forge asking, with varying degrees of embarrassment, whether she might make them something too. She spent the next week crafting what resembled bo-shuriken - straight, slender metal spikes and needles, heavily favored in traditional Japanese martial arts back on Earth - for everyone who asked, muttering the entire time about how a person could go thirty-seven years being ignored and then suddenly not get a moment's peace.

# Chapter 21

## "A Fanciful Tale"

Ars needed an audience more than he needed to be honest in front of one.

He'd gathered a knot of underclassmen near the courtyard fountain - an easy crowd, wide-eyed and new enough to the Conservatory that they hadn't yet learned which stories to trust - and was three sentences into a version of last week's practicum that bore only a glancing resemblance to what had actually happened. In Ars's telling, Alec had panicked during the fire drill, frozen up entirely, and had to be talked down by Ars himself with what he described, unblinking, as "a calm authority most seventh-years never develop."

Alec heard the last part from twenty feet away and felt something in his chest go very still and very cold, the quiet that came before he stopped being patient with people.

"That's not what happened," he said, walking into the circle without raising his voice.

Ars turned, and something flickered across his face - not quite guilt, more the irritation of an actor whose scene has been interrupted mid-monologue. "Everyone remembers it differently."

"No," Alec said. "Everyone remembers it the same way. You're the only one telling it wrong." A beat. "But sure. Let's settle it properly, since you like an audience so much."

By that evening the entire student body had somehow heard, the way word always traveled faster through the Conservatory's halls than any official notice could, and the Convocation Room filled to its rafters with students craning for a view of the dueling floor.

Most duels at the Conservatory needed no more ceremony than an empty hallway and two students with something to prove - a just-vacated classroom, the practice field, the wide stone lip of a courtyard fountain, wherever the opportunity presented itself. The one place duels never happened was the cafeteria, not because anyone had made a rule against it so much as because the staff had made the consequence very clear the first time someone tried: misused power comes with great hunger, they said, and refused to elaborate further, which taught the lesson faster than any actual explanation could have. But a formal challenge, called in front of witnesses and settled by request rather than accident, belonged to the Convocation Room - the same hall the whole student body filed through every morning for the Proclaimer's news, its floor worn into a permanent constellation of pale scars where centuries of shields and scorch marks had etched themselves into stone no repair charm ever fully lifted. A gallery ran along the upper walls, close enough to the vaulted ceiling that spectators leaned over worn stone railings polished smooth by generations of forearms, craning for the same angle their predecessors had craned for a hundred years running. At the back of the floor, raised on a low dais that put its occupant above the crowd without quite looking like a throne, sat an empty high-backed chair carved from the same stone as the rest of the hall - Grok's seat, technically, though nobody currently enrolled had ever actually seen him use it. He preferred, always, to stand beside it instead, which suited a man who watched more duels than he judged.

Fellow Aen stood at the room's center point and raised her diamond staff overhead. Rings of shimmering force bloomed outward from its tip in perfect concentric bands, each one settling into place around the gathered crowd like ripples frozen mid-spread across still water - a shield within a shield within a shield, layered thick enough to stop anything the floor might produce from reaching a single spectator. Archon Grok watched from the back with his arms folded and his golden gavel resting easy against one shoulder, the posture of a man who had seen enough duels to know exactly when to intervene and exactly when not to.

Ars went first, because Ars always went first. His wand came up, incantations spilling out of him in a rapid, showy stream. "Mini sun!" A fireball roared from the wand's tip, orange-white and furious, arcing toward Alec, who simply stepped sideways and let it detonate harmlessly against the floor's warded stone. "Cold points!" Ice daggers followed in a spinning volley, and Alec tilted his head, unhurried, letting them shatter one by one against a stray gust none of the crowd saw him summon. A cluster of wispy translucent knives came next, weaving unpredictable paths through the air - Alec caught two of them by the hilt out of sheer boredom and let the rest pass by.

Then Ars reached for something bigger. He drove his wand into the floor and dragged a great heap of the room's packed earth up into a lumbering, roughly man-shaped mass - a mud golem, eight feet tall, dripping and shapeless at the edges, arms already swinging toward Alec with blunt, crushing intent.

It kept coming. And coming. Long after any reasonable command should have redirected it, corrected it, told it anything at all beyond exist and swing, because Ars - flushed with the crowd's attention, so pleased with the theater of the thing - had forgotten, in his excitement, to actually give it an order.

Alec didn't reach for a wand he didn't own. He didn't speak a single incantation. He just walked forward, unhurried, hands loose at his sides. "You might want to witness this carefully," he said, mostly to Ars, somewhat to the crowd, "because what I'm about to do hasn't been invented yet, and it's going to ruin your afternoon entirely."

The golem folded. Not fell - folded, its entire mass turning abruptly inside out in a single wet convulsion, mud and packed earth reversing through some interior none of it should have had, and then it wasn't a golem at all anymore, it was simply momentum, and all of that momentum went into Ars.

He went down hard, and his arm - flung out instinctively to catch himself - stretched. Not broke: stretched, elongating a full impossible foot past where an arm should end, tendon and bone drawn out like taffy for one horrible suspended second before it simply came off at the shoulder with a wet, audible crack. Blood arced across the dueling floor. His chest, at the same moment, began to swell - visibly, grotesquely, ribs pushing outward as though something vast were being poured into his lungs that had nowhere left to go, because something was: water, filling him from the inside without ever touching his skin, without a drop falling anywhere Alec hadn't put it.

Grok's gavel came down once, sharp against the floor. "Winner: Alec."

The crowd didn't gasp so much as roar - not horror, astonishment, the kind of delighted disbelief a room gives an illusionist who's just done something nobody in the audience can begin to explain. They cheered the invention of it, the wrongness of it, the sheer audacity of turning a mud golem's own mass against the boy who'd forgotten to command it.

Aen was already moving before the applause finished, staff lowering, green light spilling from the diamond tip and settling over Ars like a slow tide. The swelling in his chest receded first, water finding its way back out through channels only Aen seemed to understand. His arm reattached itself with a soft, unhurried click, no scar, no seam, simply present again exactly as it had been.

"People think I mend things," she said quietly to the students pressed nearest the shield, not looking up from her work. "I don't. Every molecule in a body already knows where it belongs - injury just moves it somewhere wrong. All I do is remind it." The green light pulsed once more, and Ars's breathing evened out beneath it. "You could all learn to do this. It's not born in you. It's paid attention into you." She looked up, finally, at the rows of stunned faces ringing the floor. "Something to consider, before you all decide fireballs are the more interesting subject."

## "Dwargon Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgochdrwsyr"

It was Rorie who ended up explaining it, mostly because Brannock had a mouth full of ale and Dwargon himself had wandered off to arm-wrestle someone by the fire pit, leaving a gap in the conversation that Rorie filled without being asked.

Dwarves at the Conservatory never much bothered with the human common rooms, and it wasn't hard to see why once you'd spent an evening in the corner they'd claimed for themselves instead - a stretch of open courtyard behind the Residence Hall that had, over the years, accumulated a fire pit nobody remembered building, ringed by benches and low tables hewn from raw stone with the blunt, confident joinery of people who distrusted anything held together by glue or magic when a good chisel would do. Carved reliefs ran along the benches' undersides where somebody, at some point, had gotten bored and started decorating furniture nobody official had ever approved: hammers, tankards, a recurring motif of a dwarf mid-laugh that nobody had ever successfully identified. Kegs appeared with a regularity the kitchen staff had long since stopped questioning, tapped and refilled by hands that never seemed to belong to anyone currently visible, and once the ale started flowing the volume in that corner of the courtyard climbed until it could be heard clear across the practice yard - which was, more or less, the entire difference between a dwarf gathering and a human one. Where the Student Common Room ran on quiet conversation and careful volume control, the fire pit ran on noise as a form of affection: shouting was how you said you liked someone, arm-wrestling was how you settled anything that mattered, and silence around the fire meant something had actually gone wrong.

"You want to know about the name," he said to Kiyo Takahashi, an exchange student down from the eastern Demesnes who'd been staring at Dwargon's back like she was trying to memorize a very long word before it got away from her. "Everybody wants to know about the name eventually."

"It's just - that's a lot of name," she said. "For one dwarf."

"He's a strong leader," Rorie said, as though this were self-evidently the answer to her question and required no further scaffolding. "The strongest." He said it the way you'd state the boiling point of water - not praise, just fact, filed and settled long ago. "Every syllable's earned. That's how it works with us. You don't get handed a name that long. You grow into it, one clause at a time, and the mountain decides when you've grown enough."

"So it means something? Like, each part translates to--"

"Don't," Rorie said, holding up a hand with real warning in it. "Don't ask what it translates to. That's a whole different conversation and none of us agree on the ending."

From across the fire pit, Dwargon must have caught the shape of his own name in the air, because he straightened up mid-arm-wrestle, grinning through his beard, and called out something in a low rolling cadence - and that was all it took. Every dwarf within earshot, a dozen of them at least, scattered across benches and firelight and half-finished mugs, rose to it in a single breath, like the sound had been sitting coiled in their chests waiting for the cue.

They chanted it. Full name, start to end, in perfect unbroken unison - Dwargon Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgochdrwsyr - every syllable given exactly the same weight as the one before it and the one after, no rise, no fall, no pause where an outsider's ear kept expecting one. To Kiyo it sounded like thirty syllables strung end to end with nowhere to breathe. To every dwarf chanting it, it was one word - had always been one word, would always be one word, the way a mountain is one mountain regardless of how many ridges you can count along its spine.

Dwargon let it go on a full three rounds before he raised both hands, laughing, and the chant broke apart into cheering and mugs banged against the table in ragged, satisfied rhythm.

"See?" Rorie said, settling back onto his bench, entirely unbothered, as if he'd just demonstrated a simple point of arithmetic. "One word. Say it fast enough and it stops sounding long at all."

Kiyo looked at her own name tag, short enough to fit in one corner of it, and then back at Dwargon, who was already back to arm-wrestling with the air of a man for whom being roared at by his own gang was simply Tuesday. "And yours?" she asked Rorie.

"Rorie," he said. "Just Rorie. My family never did anything worth a second syllable." He didn't sound bothered by it in the slightest. "Give it time, though. Dwargon's granddad started at four syllables too."

# Chapter 23

## "Ars' Theory of Anchors"

The common room at seven o'clock had the hush of a study group that hadn't quite decided to be one yet - chairs pulled into a loose circle, mugs of coffee going lukewarm, Ars standing at the head of it with a stack of parchment thick enough to double as a doorstop.

Summer was doing most of the room's work for it - the last low gold of the evening still leaning in through the windows, thick enough that nobody had bothered switching on more than the recessed downlights doing quiet backup duty in the ceiling. The walls carried a scattershot history of whoever had lived in this common room before this year's occupants: movie posters from both worlds tacked up in overlapping layers, mostly Earth robots and Aeriden action-serials nobody quite believed, because a story about magic never sat right with people who did it for a living. The corners told a quieter story than the walls. Empty mugs collected by the little kitchenette, a coffee machine buried under a small landfill of spent pods, a mini fridge humming along with milk and sodas inside it, and several couches arranged around a knee-high table in the kind of comfortable disorder that came from nobody wanting to be the one who cared enough to fix it. Ars's corner of the room was the exception, and it gave him away completely: his stack of parchment squared to the table's edge, his own mug set down on a coaster nobody else in the room had ever seen used, every page he'd brought organized in an order only he could have imposed on it. Alec's half of the couch held nothing at all beyond the cup in his hand - no notes, no preparation, nothing that suggested he'd walked in expecting to need anything, because as far as he was concerned he hadn't.

"Before we start on drills," he said, "I want to share something I've been working on. Something a bit more foundational than homework."

He glanced at Alec, an old habit reasserting itself before he could stop it - checking, the way he always did, whether Alec had actually followed his rule about the coffee this time. Alec answered with a nod and a small lift of his cup, store-bought, already half gone. No wandering off tonight looking for a machine.

Aeribeth, sprawled sideways across an armchair with one leg over the arm, raised an eyebrow at Aerilyn without saying anything. Aerilyn didn't return it - she was watching Ars with the careful, unreadable attention she gave anything that might turn out to matter.

Two of the underclassmen Ars had roped in for the group exchanged a glance that suggested they'd already learned to brace themselves.

"It's called," Ars announced, turning the title page outward with the reverence of a man unveiling a monument, "*A Unified Theory of Magical Anchoring: Toward a Complete Taxonomy of Channeled Intent.*"

"Catchy," Alec said, from the couch, not looking up from where he was balancing a pencil on one knuckle.

Ars either didn't hear the dryness in it or chose not to. He cleared his throat and began to read. "'It is the central thesis of this treatise that all magical practice, without exception, requires the presence of dual anchoring mechanisms - a physical conduit and a verbal formalization - operating in necessary and sufficient concert. Apparent deviations from this principle are best understood not as counterexamples, but as instances of anchoring so deeply internalized as to escape casual observation.'"

He looked up, visibly pleased with himself. "I workshopped that sentence for two days."

"It shows," Aeribeth said, entirely deadpan, and one of the underclassmen made a small strangled noise into their coffee.

Ars pressed on, undeterred, reading through a section on standard casting theory that was, to be fair, mostly correct - the wand-and-word framework he'd grown up inside, described with genuine precision. It was the leap after that made Alec set the pencil down.

"'Where a practitioner appears to cast without formal tool or incantation,'" Ars read, "'closer analysis reveals that the anchor has simply migrated inward, becoming so total and unconscious that the practitioner mistakes its absence for its nonexistence. True anchor-independence is, strictly speaking, a category error - a misunderstanding of one's own mechanism, not an absence of mechanism.'"

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when everyone present knows exactly who that sentence is about and is waiting to see whether he'll notice.

"Ars," Alec said, gently enough that it took the room a second to register it wasn't the setup for a joke. "You're describing me right now. In a paper. Without saying my name."

"I'm describing a phenomenon," Ars said. "You happen to be the most visible instance of it."

"Right, but here's my question." Alec sat forward, elbows on his knees, and there was nothing performative in it - no dry wit waiting behind his eyes, just genuine attention. "You've watched me work for over a year. You've seen me cast without ever once reaching for something that wasn't there to begin with. What made you decide that what you were watching wasn't real, instead of just... not yet explained?"

"Because the alternative doesn't hold together," Ars said, some of his usual polish thinning under the direct question. "If magic doesn't require an anchor, then the entire framework - everything the Conservatory teaches, everything standard casting is built on - is just one option among many with no unifying principle behind it. That can't be right. There has to be a rule underneath all of it."

"Maybe," Alec said. "Or maybe the rule is just smaller than you want it to be. You've got one example - you - and you're building the whole universe out of it. That's not really a theory. That's just describing yourself really precisely and calling it everyone."

Ars's jaw tightened. "It's not just me. Every mage I've ever studied under-"

"Uses wand and incantation, sure," Alec said. "Because that's who they are. But Aerilyn's right there." He nodded toward her without turning to look. "She channels through daggers she pulls out of her own skin. That's not wand-and-word. That's not even close to your framework, and you've watched her do it in front of you for a year too."

Aerilyn didn't say anything, but she held very still, the way she did when she wasn't sure whether being used as an example was a compliment or an inconvenience.

"And you've heard about Tyler," Alec went on, quieter now, choosing the words with more care than he usually bothered with. "And Aen. I'm not going to explain what they do - it's not mine to explain - but you know enough to know it doesn't fit your paper either. None of us fit the same shape. That's not a flaw in the data, Ars. That might just be what magic actually is."

Alec reached again.

Alec reached a third time.

The reach that had always simply worked, the way his lungs worked, the way his heart worked, met something on the way out and came back empty, like a hand thrust into a drawer that used to hold something and now held only the memory of its shape. Magic left him the way breathing left him - no ceremony, no cost, nothing to earn. He understood, standing gray-faced and swaying in an alley that smelled like copper and fear, that effortless had always been a story he'd been telling himself about a debt he hadn't yet been asked to pay.

He went down mid-swing, weapon and all, a shout swallowed by the dark before it finished leaving his throat. ## "Nothing Needed"

Ahead, Tyler raised a fist. The group slowed, folding into the shadow of the last wall before the compound's heart.

Ars matched their pace exactly, wand steady in his hand, and did not fall behind again.

## "Leaving"

## "The Toll of Esyrhcne"

"Effective, though." She held out the other half of the toast. After a moment, he took it.

## "Aerilyn's First Absorption Test"

She hadn't been back since. Thirteen years, and Aerilyn had never once found a reason - or maybe never let herself find one - to walk the trail back into the Terradin wilds to the place where all of it had started. Not until you've had a chance to show her first."

Aerilyn opened one eye, looking down at the black ink covering her arms - not ordinary tattoo-ink black anymore, she realized, but something with a faint, self-luminous shine to it even in the clearing's golden afternoon light.

"I'm walking around wearing five Aebgarmr's worth of teeth," she said slowly.

"You are." Aeribeth grinned, standing and offering a hand down to pull her up off the moss. "C'mon. Let's go make a dwarf's whole year. I want to watch Rorie's face when she sees you walk in."

# Chapter 43

## "The Fracture on Earth"

Alec had fully recovered. His hands didn't shake anymore, his color had come back, and Aen had pronounced him, with the satisfaction of someone closing out a difficult case, entirely whole. But not everything healed at the same speed, and some mornings he still woke expecting the gray, reaching for it out of habit before remembering it was gone.

It was on one of those mornings that the Proclaimer's Special Edition chime cut through the Convocation Room, sharper than usual, and two hundred students who had learned over the past months exactly what that pitch meant went quiet before the scroll had even finished unrolling.

"Hear ye, hear ye. Reports from Los Angeles - the Earth Technologists Front has fractured."

The room stirred. Alec, near the back, felt Aerilyn go still beside him the way she always did when something mattered enough to actually listen to.

"With Malakai Sterling's disappearance," the Proclaimer continued, eyes on her scroll, "command of the Front has splintered into competing factions. Some voices call for closure - an end to hostilities, a return to the fragile peace that existed before his rise. Others see opportunity in the chaos, moving to seize what authority remains. And some, by every account gathered, seem simply to have lost the thread of what they were fighting for in the first place, continuing forward on inertia alone, unable to say what winning would even look like anymore."

She paused, the way she always paused before the part of the story that actually had a shape to it.

"One incident, three days past, has drawn particular attention."

---

The order had come down through channels that were, strictly speaking, no longer channels at all - a splinter faction operating out of a warehouse two blocks from the Pacific Coast Highway 1 crossing, still wearing Technologist insignia, still issuing directives as though the chain of command that had once given those directives weight hadn't simply evaporated along with the man at the top of it.

The warehouse gave up nothing of what it had once stored - just dust now, propane residue from forklifts long since repossessed, crates of unsold inventory nobody had bothered clearing out before the lease lapsed into whatever legal gray area a splinter cell operates from. What furniture there was had been dragged in rather than built for the purpose: folding tables scavenged from somewhere, a bank of radios that looked less like military-grade equipment and more like whatever a hardware store still had in stock, wired together with a patience that suggested nobody left in the group actually understood the systems Sterling's engineers had once run this same operation on. A framed photograph of Sterling still hung near the makeshift command table, corners curling, propped rather than mounted, alongside a stenciled Technologist banner nobody had gotten around to taking down even though the man it answered to no longer gave orders anyone could verify. It had the look of an operation running on inertia rather than leadership - insignia, jargon, and the shape of a chain of command, none of it actually connected to anything at the top anymore, kept alive by people who needed the structure to still mean something more than they needed it to actually be true.
Sweep the portal district. Detain anyone reading maged on the scanners. Hold them for processing.

Nobody had asked processing by whom, or under what authority, or toward what end. That was the trouble with orders that outlived the reason for giving them - they kept moving long after the hand that had first pushed them forward had stopped existing to answer for it.

Officer Miao received the directive over a channel he technically still monitored out of professional courtesy more than obligation, sitting astride his Hovercat at the edge of the crossing in the gray hour before dawn, and read it twice before he understood that he was being asked to participate in something that had no warrant behind it, no court order, no judge - holographic or otherwise - who would ever review the case, because there was no case. Just men with leftover authority and nowhere left to point it.

The portal district at this hour belonged to nobody in particular. A thin queue had already formed along the checkpoint's approach - legitimate travelers mostly, papers in hand, patient in the specific way of people who had learned that patience was the only currency a checkpoint actually accepted, though tonight it was thinner than usual, padded out by a scattering of protesters who'd been camped on the sidewalks for days now, sleeping in shifts against a crisis nobody in charge seemed able to name. Customs agents worked the scanner gates in their usual rotation, waving through anyone whose readout came back clean, while overhead the arch's etched runes pulsed their familiar faint gold at anything that didn't. It was, on its surface, a morning like any other morning at any other portal crossing - Earth's machinery and Aeriden's warding running side by side the way they always had, indifferent to whatever chain of command was currently falling apart three blocks away.

He watched, from a distance, as a truck rolled up to the crossing and six men in tactical gear began pulling frightened travelers out of the queue - a family of four, an elderly man with a Proclaimer's satchel still slung over one shoulder, a young woman who hadn't done anything more threatening than exist on the wrong side of a scanner's readout.

Miao had spent fifteen years filing reports in perfect chronological order, closing cases with the tidy, unhurried precision of a man who trusted the system to be worth trusting. He thought about that, briefly, watching the young woman get shoved toward the truck bed. He thought about how much of his career had depended on believing that the badge meant something specific and bounded, that authority came with an edge past which it simply stopped being authority at all.

He engaged the Hovercat's transformation sequence before he'd finished deciding to.

Twelve seconds. Plating unfolded around him in a single fluid ripple, and by the time he crossed the distance to the truck the exoskeleton had fully seated itself, his stance settling into the same balanced readiness that had carried him through fifteen years of roadside stops that had never once required him to raise his voice to be believed.

"Stand down," he said. Not loud. He had never needed loud.

The lead officer - if officer was even the word anymore, Miao thought, watching the man's insignia catch the pre-dawn light with nothing behind it to back the claim up - turned with the irritation of someone unused to being questioned. "This isn't your jurisdiction, Sergeant."

"It's exactly my jurisdiction. This crossing's been mine for six years." Miao's voice carried the same flat, unhurried cadence it always did, the one that had talked down men holding weapons far more dangerous than the situation currently in front of him. "You want to tell me what court authorized this detention? What judge signed off? Because I've got a system on this bike that projects three holographic magistrates in under a second, and none of them are going to find a warrant that doesn't exist."

"We don't need a warrant. We're finishing what Sterling started."

"Sterling's gone," Miao said. "And whatever authority he had went with him. You're not finishing anything. You're just men in a truck, grabbing people who haven't broken a single law, because nobody's told you to stop yet." He let that sit a beat, watching it land. "I'm telling you to stop."

The standoff held for three long seconds - long enough for the young woman to twist free of the grip on her arm and scramble back toward the crossing, long enough for the elderly man to clutch his Proclaimer's satchel tighter against his chest, long enough for Miao to understand, with the clarity of a man who had made peace with a decision before his conscious mind had finished catching up to it, that he was not backing down regardless of what came next.

The lead officer's hand drifted toward his own sidearm.

Miao didn't move. He didn't need to. The Hovercat's forearm plating hummed with a slow, deliberate charge that needed no further explanation, and something in the calculation behind the other man's eyes visibly shifted - the arithmetic of a man realizing, in real time, that he was outmatched by someone who had never once raised his voice.

The truck left without its cargo. Nobody was detained that morning at the Pacific Coast Highway 1 crossing.

Miao filed his report before his shift ended, same as he always did, in perfect chronological order - though this was the first report in fifteen years he genuinely wasn't certain would be read the way he meant it, by anyone with the authority left to read it at all.

---

Dr. Priya Malhotra read it within the hour.

She had spent the chaotic weeks since Sterling's disappearance doing what she had always done - gathering data, cataloguing incidents, building the kind of careful, impeccable record that had made her credible even to people who fundamentally disagreed with her about what mages were and what they deserved. She had watched the Front fracture with the clinical, exhausted patience of someone who had predicted this outcome months ago and taken no satisfaction whatsoever in being right.

She cited Miao's report publicly the following day, in a statement carried by every major feed on the West Coast - not as an isolated act of one officer's conscience, but as evidence, data, exactly the kind of concrete example she had spent her career building arguments around.

"Officer Miao's actions this morning represent exactly the standard this movement claimed, at its founding, to want," she said, her voice carrying the same measured, unshakeable precision that had made her impossible to dismiss for years. "Oversight. Accountability. Rules that apply even when it is inconvenient to follow them. What happened at that crossing was not law enforcement. It was men with leftover authority deciding, on their own, that the rules no longer applied to them. Officer Miao reminded them that rules exist precisely because they are inconvenient - that is the entire point of having them."

She paused, and something almost personal crept into her otherwise clinical tone. "If the Earth Technologists Front wants to survive Malakai Sterling's fall as anything other than the thing it accused mages of being - unaccountable, unregulated, dangerous by design - it will look to officers like this one, and not to the men in that truck."

The statement traveled fast. By the end of the week, Miao's name was known in circles he'd never once expected to reach him - mages he'd never met thanking him through channels that reached the portal district in whispers and folded notes, ordinary Earthers who'd watched the footage and found something in it worth holding onto during a season when very little else felt stable. He didn't seek any of it out. He filed his next report the same way he'd filed every report before it, in perfect chronological order, and went back to watching the crossing at dawn, same as always.

---

The Proclaimer rolled her scroll closed, the Convocation Room settling back into its ordinary hum of two hundred separate conversations.

"Sources indicate," she added, almost as an afterthought, "that Dr. Malhotra's statement has shifted momentum within the Front's more moderate factions, though the situation remains, by every account, deeply unsettled."

Aerilyn glanced sideways at Alec. "Sounds like Earth's got its own version of learning what to do without someone telling them the rules anymore."

"Yeah," Alec said, and something in his face - still recovering, not fully healed, the way none of them quite were yet - eased just slightly at the thought of a stranger three thousand miles and a portal crossing away, choosing, all on his own, to stand between people and a harm nobody had authorized him to stop.

# Chapter 44

## "The Conservatory After"

Aerilyn found Alec on the same bench in the same courtyard where she'd first watched him walk past her without seeming to notice anyone was watching at all - though back then she hadn't known his name, only that something about the shape of his walk didn't match anyone else's. Weeks had passed since the Remand. His color had settled fully now, no gray left anywhere she could see, and something in the way he sat had settled too - less like a boy waiting for the next disaster, more like someone who'd finally set a weight down and trusted the ground to hold it.

"You're staring," he said, without opening his eyes.

"I'm observing. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"With you, always." She sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders found each other without either of them arranging it. "Grok's called an assembly. Full Convocation Hall. Word is someone's coming to speak."

"Someone."

"Aleron Vance."

Alec opened his eyes at that. Not surprise, exactly - more the stillness of a man recalculating the shape of something he'd only ever known as a voice on a scroll. "The man's been a rumor with excellent diction for two years. Feels strange that he's about to be a person standing in a room."

"Feels stranger that we're the reason he's coming." Aerilyn watched a knot of first-years cross the courtyard, laughing about something that had nothing to do with war or bubbles or names spelled backwards, and something in her chest ached, briefly and without explanation, at how ordinary it all looked from the outside. "You ready?"

"For Vance?"

"For any of it."

Alec considered the question with the same unhurried honesty he gave most things. "I don't know if ready's the word. I think I'm just - here. Still here. Which felt less certain a few weeks ago than it does today." He glanced at her, something easy and unguarded in it that hadn't always been there between them. "You gonna sit with me? In there?"

"Where else would I sit, Alec?"

He didn't answer that. He didn't need to. He reached over and took her hand instead - not dramatic, not announced, just a fact establishing itself the way facts sometimes did between two people who'd stopped needing to perform anything for each other - and they sat like that a while longer before the bell finally called them both inside.

---

The Convocation Hall filled the way it had filled once before, a lifetime and a war ago - vaulted stone ribs swallowing sound overhead, students finding seats by habit rather than assignment, the hush of a building that had learned, over centuries, exactly how to hold two hundred held breaths at once. But it filled differently this time. Aerilyn noticed it crossing the threshold: fewer whispered rumors, more direct, open watching. Whatever this assembly was, nobody in the room was pretending they didn't know it mattered.

The only concession to what had happened hung modestly beneath the row of stained-glass windows nearest the door: a single unadorned wreath of pressed Terradin scrub, tied off in plain cord, no plaque beneath it yet because nobody had agreed on wording that felt honest rather than convenient. There had been talk of something grander - a new bust to stand among the old archons and principals, a fresh set of names added to the wall's quiet catalogue of the dead - but Grok had overruled all of it before the ink dried on the first proposal. You didn't celebrate the end of a war while people who'd fought in it were still learning to sleep through the night, he'd said, and a war memorial unveiled before the grief had finished arriving felt less like honoring the dead than rushing past them. So the hall stayed almost exactly as it always had, and the only real difference lived in the wreath by the door and in the two hundred faces turned toward the podium with none of the performance that had filled this same room on the first morning of the year.

Archon Grok did not walk to the podium so much as arrive at it, the way weather arrives, exactly as he had on the first morning of the year the six of them had all separately walked through these gates not yet knowing what they'd become to each other. He set the golden gavel down with the same single, unhurried click, and the hall's remaining noise died before the sound had finished traveling to the back wall.

"Some of you," Grok said, without preamble, without warmth performed for its own sake, "already know most of what I'm about to say. You've heard the rumors. You've read between the lines of Proclaimer broadcasts that were, I'll admit, considerably more evasive than I'd have liked." A small, dry pause. "I don't intend to be evasive today."

He let his gaze move across the rows, the same slow, weighing attention he'd given them all on their first morning, cataloguing something in each face before moving on.

"Malakai Sterling is no longer a threat to this world or any other. I won't detail the particulars of how that came to be true, because the particulars belong to six students in this hall who earned the right to keep them, and because some truths are heavier to carry publicly than they are privately. What I will tell you is this: he faces formal reckoning before Aeriden's courts, the same as any prisoner, the same as any man whose crimes are catalogued and answered for rather than simply erased. That is not mercy. It is the opposite of what he offered anyone he considered beneath him. It is, I think, the correct ending for a man who built an empire on the belief that rules were for other people."

A murmur moved through the hall - not shock, not quite relief either, something closer to the exhale of two hundred people finally allowed to stop bracing.

"I told you, on your first morning here, that you might not leave this Conservatory the same as you entered it. Some of you have learned exactly how true that was. I will not stand here and tell you the cost was worth it in some clean, ledger-balanced way, because grief doesn't balance, and I have never respected a leader who pretends otherwise. What I will tell you is that this institution exists to produce mages who understand the weight of what they carry - and this year, six of our own carried more weight than any curriculum could have prepared them for, and set it down without letting it break them. That is not nothing. That is, in fact, everything this school has ever tried to teach."

He paused, and something almost unguarded crossed his face - not warmth exactly, but its close cousin, the recognition of a man who had watched something he'd spent thirty years hoping for actually happen in front of him.

"The Conservatory does not stop teaching what it has always taught. But it teaches it differently now, because six of you proved something the faculty has debated for years without resolving: that the space between standard magic and advanced magic is not a wall. It is a door. Some of you will spend your whole careers on one side of it. Some of you - more than we once believed possible - will find your way through." He glanced, briefly, toward the row where Alec, Aerilyn, Aeribeth, Ars, Tyler, and Aen sat together. "We intend to teach toward that door from now on, rather than pretend it doesn't exist."

He lifted the gavel, turned it once so the gold caught the high windows' pale light, and set it down again.

"There is someone else who asked to speak today. I granted the request, because I believe what he has to say matters more than my continuing to occupy this podium." A ghost of dry humor crossed his face. "Also because he's rather difficult to say no to, which those of you who've heard him broadcast will already understand."

---

The shimmer that announced a portal opened at the hall's side entrance, and Aleron Vance walked through it the way he apparently did everything else - without asking permission, without needing to.

He was smaller than Aerilyn had somehow imagined, and larger at the same time, the contradiction of someone whose presence occupied more space than his actual body did. Silver-streaked dark hair swept back from a high forehead; a long charcoal coat that had clearly seen more distance than comfort; a walking cane, aethelite-tipped, that he planted once on the stone floor with a sound that somehow carried to every corner of the hall despite being nothing more than wood meeting stone.

He didn't go to the podium. He stopped a few feet short of it, close enough to the assembled students that the distance itself felt like a deliberate choice, and looked out over two hundred faces with the same unhurried, absolute certainty that had made his broadcasts stop entire city squares mid-conversation.

"I've spoken to this world three times now through a Proclaimer's scroll," he said, and his voice needed no amplification at all, carrying to the back wall with the same effortless authority it had carried across Oserean's central plaza. "Each time, I told you Sterling had to be stopped. Each time, I meant it, and each time I had no idea who would actually be the ones to do it." He let that settle. "I do now."

He turned, deliberately, toward the row of six.

"I have spent thirty years in rooms where the stakes were measured in treaties and trade agreements and the exhaustion of men who thought they held all the power in the world. I have never, in thirty years, watched six people do what the six of you did at that FOB." He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to; the hall had gone entirely, breathlessly still. "You ended a war that an army of ten thousand men and twelve warships could not win for the other side. You did it without becoming what he was. That distinction matters more than anyone in this hall may yet understand, but I promise you it will matter more, not less, as the years go on."

He planted the cane again, and something in his expression shifted - the diplomat giving way, briefly, to something plainer and more direct.

"The League of Magic is not going anywhere now that Sterling's gone. If anything, his fall has made clearer what should have been clear from the start: that Aeriden needs something more permanent than a coalition assembled in crisis and disbanded the moment the crisis passes. We are building toward that. Slowly. Carefully. The way anything worth building actually gets built."

His eyes moved across the six of them, one at a time, unhurried. "I am not here to conscript anyone. You have all more than earned the right to walk away from every version of this fight that isn't the one directly in front of you. But if any of you - together, separately, whenever you're ready, if you're ever ready - want a door into what comes next for this world, I am telling you, plainly, that the door exists. It has your names already written beside it, whether or not you ever choose to open it."

He inclined his head, the smallest possible bow, and stepped back.

"That's all I came to say. The rest of the ceremony, I understand, belongs to Archon Grok and to all of you." A faint, genuine smile. "I've taken up enough of a Tuesday."

---

The formal portion of the assembly wound down not long after - announcements about the coming term, a moment of silence Grok called for the students and faculty who hadn't made it through the year, the ordinary business of a school continuing to be a school even after everything. But the six of them lingered afterward, in the thinning crowd, in the way people linger when a room has held something too large to simply walk away from immediately.

Ars found Alec first, an old, familiar habit reasserting itself, except something in how he approached had changed - less bluster in it, more of the steadiness that had been growing in him since a night he still hadn't fully talked about with anyone. "I revised the treatise," he said, without preamble. "Properly this time. Not the language. The thesis."

"Yeah?"

"There isn't one universal anchor. There's a spectrum, and most people sit somewhere on it they didn't choose, and a handful of people don't sit on it at all." Ars said it plainly, without the old defensiveness, like a man reading out a fact rather than defending a position. "I used you as the primary case study. And Aerilyn. I cited you both by name. I thought you should know before it goes to the Conservatory archive, since apparently I'm capable of being wrong in print now and surviving it."

Alec studied him for a moment, something warm and entirely without mockery in his expression. "You didn't need my permission."

"I know. I wanted you to have it anyway." Ars shrugged, an easy, settled gesture that had nothing performative left in it at all. "Turns out being the wrench instead of the sword doesn't actually feel like losing, once you stop keeping score against people who were never in your event to begin with."

Nearby, Tyler stood at the edge of a knot of first-years who'd gathered to ask him something about the coming term's schedule, and Aeribeth watched him field their questions with an ease that hadn't been there a year ago - no hand drifting toward his wand out of habit, no uncertainty about whether he belonged behind whatever podium he found himself near. He caught her watching and crossed over once the students had drifted off.

"You look like you've got something to say," he told her.

"You look like you finally believe you're allowed to be here," she said. "Teaching, I mean. Not just standing near it."

Something eased in his face, quiet and real. "Working on it."

"Looks done to me."

Aen stood a little apart from the rest, staff dark and quiet at her side, and it was Aerilyn who crossed to her first - not out of duty, but out of the gravity that had drawn the two of them together since the first time Aerilyn had watched her rearrange a wound that shouldn't have been fixable. "You never let anyone take care of you," Aerilyn said, without preamble. "The whole year. Not once."

"That's not my role."

"Today it is." Aerilyn didn't ask permission. She simply took Aen's free hand in both of hers, the way Aen had once held Alec's shoulders in green light without asking, and held it there a moment, offering back some fraction of what had been given all year without ever once being repaid in kind. "You saved every one of us. More than once. Somebody should say it plainly instead of just being alive because of it."

Aen's composure, so carefully maintained through a war and its aftermath, wavered for just a moment - something bright and unguarded surfacing before she pulled it back into its familiar shape. "I don't entirely know where it comes from," she admitted, quiet enough that it was almost just for Aerilyn. "The healing. I've never asked. Never had a reason to, until recently." She glanced, briefly, toward Aerilyn's forearms, still faintly luminous beneath her sleeves. "Maybe I will now."

Aeribeth found Aerilyn last, the two of them drifting toward the hall's tall doors while the others said their own goodbyes behind them, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all - just walked, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd walked more afternoons than either of them could count across thirteen years.

"You don't need me the way you used to," Aeribeth said finally, and there was no grief in it, only a kind of clear-eyed honesty that had always been the truest thing about her. "Haven't for a while now, if I'm being honest with myself."

"That's not the same as not needing you."

"I know." Aeribeth smiled, small and real. "Just took me thirteen years to stop needing to be needed the specific way I used to be. Turns out there's other ways to matter to someone. Standing beside them instead of in front of them, mostly." She bumped Aerilyn's shoulder with her own. "Grok mentioned something to Dwargon, by the way. About formalizing Demesne liaison work. Somebody who bridges the Conservatory and the Terradin wilds properly, instead of just showing up whenever there's chaos to sort out."

"They asked you?"

"They're going to." Aeribeth's grin widened. "I already know what I'm saying."

---

The two moons had begun their slow climb over the Conservatory's towers by the time the six of them finally stepped out into the courtyard together, the Convocation Hall emptying behind them into ordinary evening noise - students trading plans for dinner, someone laughing too loud near the fountain, the whole enormous machinery of a school simply continuing to be a school, the way it always would, the way it had before any of them arrived and would long after they were gone.

The belltower over the west wall held the day's last light longest, gold going to amber going to the deep blue that came right before real dark, and here and there across the courtyard a head turned as the six of them crossed it - not staring, nothing so obvious as that, just a small nod from a stranger near the fountain, a quiet dip of the chin from a knot of third-years who'd clearly heard some version of what happened even if none of the details lined up twice. Nobody stopped them to ask. Nobody needed to. It was the kind of recognition that didn't require a story attached to it, just the simple, settled fact that something had been kept safe, and that the people responsible were now walking home through the same ordinary evening as everyone else.

Alec's hand found Aerilyn's again without either of them announcing it. Ars fell into step beside Tyler, still talking - genuinely talking, not performing - about some theoretical wrinkle in anchor spectrum he wanted a second opinion on. Aen walked a little lighter than she had in weeks, staff quiet at her side, something settled and curious in her expression that hadn't been there before today. Aeribeth walked backward for a few steps, facing the rest of them, grinning at nothing in particular, before turning to fall into her usual place beside Aerilyn.

Above them, Oserean's rooftops caught the last violet edge of daylight, and somewhere beyond the walls the wider world kept turning - a man facing trial in a stone room he'd never expected to see the inside of, a League of Magic quietly becoming something more permanent than a crisis, a highway crossing three thousand miles and one portal away where an officer was, at that very moment, filing another report in perfect chronological order, unaware that his name had reached a Conservatory courtyard he would never visit.

None of them knew yet what came next - not really, not in any way that could be planned for or studied toward. That had never been the lesson this place actually taught. The lesson had always been smaller and larger than that at once: that the space between what you were handed and what you became was never a wall. It was a door. Some people spent their whole lives standing in front of it, deciding.

Six of them had already walked through.

They crossed the courtyard together as the second moon cleared the tower line, six shapes moving unhurried through blue-violet dusk, already changed, still changing, carrying between them everything this year had cost and everything it had, somehow, against every reasonable expectation, given back.
